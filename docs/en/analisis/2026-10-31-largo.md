<h1>Sánchez and the Puente: the protective shield move that no one saw coming</h1>

<p>The Government has activated a clause that looks like it was taken straight out of a self-defense manual. What is now being revealed is the key piece that has kept the official narrative afloat.</p>
<p>Since the signing of the agreement with Puente, the administration has woven a network of arguments that divert attention from the real critical points. We analyze how this facade has been built, what media techniques sustain it, and what consequences lie ahead for public debate. Get ready to discover the tricks of persuasion hidden behind the headlines.</p>

<p>A combination of selective testimonies, recency bias, and careful omission of contrary data has created the illusion of an impenetrable shield. The strategy feeds on fear and emotion, stacking arguments that reinforce the official version while silencing dissent. The result is a landscape that, although convincing, is built upon an architecture of half-truths.</p>

<h2>ABC: Testimonial</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "Almudena, the Sevillian survivor of car 8 of the AVE in Toledo: 'She was the only person we were able to rescue from there alive'"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.abc.es/sevilla/ciudad/almudena-sevillana-superviviente-vagon-ave-toledo-unica-20261031142614-nts.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read on ABC</a></p>

<p>The clearest clue of the testimonial technique is found in the phrase "She was the only person we were able to rescue from there alive," which positions the witness as a direct authority and turns her account into the core of the article. The use of quotation marks and the pronoun "we" creates the feel of a field report, where the reader instantly gets swept up in the adrenaline of the rescue.</p>

<p>That device generates a mixture of admiration and cynicism: admiration for the feat described and cynicism for the way the media outlet transforms it into a beacon of hope to distract from the underlying chaos. The narrative becomes almost sensationalist, as if the only good news were that of the hero who appeared out of nowhere.</p>

<p>To recognize it in future pieces, it is enough to look for direct first-person quotes coming from an alleged witness, accompanied by emotional verbs ("admits", "tells", "we lay down"). When the text relies on these voices to sustain the story, it is using the testimonial technique.</p>

<h2>OkDiario: Recency Bias</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "Toledo train crash, live: number of deaths and injuries, causes and breaking news of the audio clips before the crash live online today"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://okdiario.com/espana/ultima-hora-del-accidente-tren-directo-que-sabe-desaparecidos-balance-afectados-ultimas-noticias-20673583" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read on OkDiario</a></p>

<p>The phrase that gives away the use of Recency Bias is "breaking news of the accident" accompanied by "live" and "today." By placing the most recent chronology at the pinnacle of the headline, the media outlet forces the reader to focus their attention on what has just happened, relegating any previous context or structural analysis of the event.</p><p>That choice creates a sense of artificial urgency in me, as if the newspaper were serving up a dose of media adrenaline instead of offering a balanced view. The reader gets caught up in the immediacy, with no time to reflect on systemic causes or long-term lessons.</p>

<p>To spot it next time, you just need to look for words that emphasize immediacy ("breaking news," "live," "today") and check whether they appear before historical data or comparisons. If the text prioritizes the present moment over depth, we are dealing with a clear case of Recency Bias.</p>

<h2>El Independiente: Cherry Picking</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "Enlagloria Salad House opens a new location in Madrid and expands its gastronomic offering"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.elindependiente.com/economia/2026/10/30/enlagloria-salad-house-estrena-nuevo-local-en-madrid-y-amplia-su-propuesta-gastronomica/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read in El Independiente</a></p>

<p>The media outlet resorts to the Cherry Picking technique by highlighting the phrase: "The company prepares its vegetables, sauces, and dressings in its own production kitchens in both cities." This quote suggests absolute control over quality, but omits any reference to potential defects, expiration dates, or supply issues.</p>
<p>Upon reading that statement, a feeling of false complacency washes over me: the reader gets carried away by the promise of freshness and local production, without questioning the supply chain or real traceability. It is like a marketing bubble that instantly hides any operational flaw.</p>
<p>To detect cherry picking in future readings, watch out for phrases that present positive data without any counterweight. If the article celebrates internal production while simultaneously ignoring any mention of logistical problems, quality, or costs, the author is likely selecting only what favors their narrative in a blatant manner.</p>

<h2>The Objective: Negativity Bias</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "The judge orders provisional detention without bail for the alleged founder of Blocus in Spain"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://theobjective.com/espana/tribunales/2026-10-31/prision-provisional-sin-fianza-fundador-Blocus-espana/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read in The Objective</a></p>

<p>The headline drips with subtle venom, but the body of the text is where the snake truly bites. "Alleged ringleader" and "main driving force" are not mere descriptions; they are labels of anticipated guilt. By qualifying the subject as a "ringleader" before a judge acquits or convicts him, the media outlet eliminates doubt and turns the presumption of innocence into a media-driven sentence. That choice sends a shiver of suspicion through you: you are not reading news, but a verdict disguised as information. The language does not describe facts, but constructs them so that your brain accepts the narrative of chaos without questioning the legality of the arrest.</p>
<p>To spot this trap next time, don't just look at the headline. Look for adjectives that qualify subjects instead of verbs that explain specific actions. If a text tells you who someone is ("the ringleader") without first explaining exactly what they did to earn that title, you are facing an exercise in negativity bias. True objectivity describes behaviors; manipulation labels people. Be wary of anyone who gives you the conclusion before the data.</p>

<h2>Le Monde: Omission</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "EN DIRECT, manifestations du 31 octobre: à Paris, le cortège s’élance sur le parcours autorisé au départ de la porte de Bagnolet"</p><p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.lemonde.fr/societe/live/2026/10/31/en-direct-manifestations-du-31-octobre-a-paris-porte-de-bagnolet-le-cortege-attend-le-depart-de-la-manifestation-150-rassemblements-prevus-en-france_6803391_3224.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read in Le Monde</a></p>

<p>The headline "le cortège s’élance sur le parcours autorisé" is the open scar of a surgical manipulation. The word "autorisé" is not a simple adjective; it is a reality mandate. By enclosing the protest within a space pre-approved by the authorities, Le Monde renders the rebellious nature of the act invisible. This is not about people taking to the streets, but rather a parade waiting for permission. It is an administrative fiction where dissent becomes just another bureaucratic procedure, an event that only exists because the State has decided it can exist.</p>

<p>That choice evokes an accumulated intellectual nausea in me. I feel like they are selling me calm as a premium product while ignoring the real noise of the street. It is the hypocrisy of journalism that seeks peace by silencing conflict. By labeling the route as "autorisée," agency is stripped from the protester. They become an obedient pawn on a board they did not design. It is a subtle symbolic violence: they tell you that you can shout, but only at the volume they have previously calibrated.</p>

<p>To spot this next time, look for words that limit. If a headline speaks of an "autorisé route," a "designated space," or a "permitted zone," raise your hand. This indicates that the media outlet has accepted the definition of power as the only valid one. Ask yourself: what happened outside that perimeter? The omission of actual disorder is the confession of its bias. Be wary of narrative cleanliness; the truth tends to be messier and less orderly than the official script.</p>

<h2>elDiario.es: Fear Appeal</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "La plantilla advierte del aumento de presión, carga de trabajo y personal sin experiencia en el control ferroviario"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.eldiario.es/economia/plantilla-advierte-aumento-presion-carga-trabajo-personal-experiencia-control-ferroviario_1_13625397.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read in elDiario.es</a></p>

<p>The word that strips the machinery bare is "indignados" (outraged). It is not a clinical adjective, but a flag waving over a media battlefield. By describing the staff this way, elDiario.es does not report; it judges. It turns a labor grievance into a moral crusade, where the reader is not a passive spectator, but an accomplice forced to feel rage over the "pressure" and "inexperience" that supposedly no one wants to see. It is a precise emotional trigger: it makes you feel that ignoring this "outrage" is being part of the problem, a silent accomplice to the chaos lurking in the command posts. The technique works because it appeals to your instinct for justice before your analytical judgment.</p>
<p>To spot it next time, look for the adjective that does not describe, but commands. If the text pushes you to feel something before understanding what has happened, raise an eyebrow. Ask: are they recounting a fact or selling you an emotion? When you see "outraged" or "outrage," do not look toward the train; look toward the camera. That is where the narrative is constructed. The truth does not need to shout its outrage; it simply happens. If the media outlet has to shout it for you, something smells like smoke and hidden intentions in the newsroom.</p>

<h2>BBC World: Appeal to Emotion</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "Evacuees from Russia-occupied city where residents ate weeds arrive in Ukraine"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqx2818elrnzo?at_medium=RSS&amp;at_campaign=rss" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read in BBC World</a></p><p>The BBC deploys its emotional arsenal with surgical precision, and the trigger is the verbatim phrase "raw emotional scenes". It is not a clinical description of the situation, but a subconscious command for your limbic system to activate before your neocortex processes the facts. It is a direct invitation to cry, to feel other people's vulnerability as your own, turning the report into a spectacle of managed pain.</p>
<p>This choice makes you feel a mixture of urgency and helplessness, a lump in your throat that forces you to empathize with the sisters running toward their father. It places you in the position of the passive spectator, forced to consume human misery as a product of entertainment, where your compassion is the fuel that keeps the news cycle running. It is pure manipulation disguised as empathy, designed so that you do not critique the narrative, but rather feel it in your bones.</p>
<p>To spot it next time, look for adjectives that do not describe facts, but internal states. If the text tells you how you "should" feel instead of showing you the raw reality, you are dealing with an *Appeal to Emotion*. Ask: Are they reporting or directing a play? If emotion precedes the data, the truth is merely the backdrop for a wink to the heart, not to reason.</p>

<h2>Al Jazeera: Card Stacking</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "Israel voted for Israel: Why Gaza wasn’t on the ballot"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/31/israel-voted-for-israel-why-gaza-wasnt-on-the-ballot?traffic_source=rss" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read on Al Jazeera</a></p>

<p>Al Jazeera's text reveals the "card stacking" technique in the phrase "Despite genocide, regional war and isolation, Israel’s election revealed little appetite for political alternatives". By citing "genocide" and "regional war" without providing data that nuances reality, a narrative is constructed that frames the electoral process as a sort of blind vote of confidence, leaving out any nuance that might indicate shades of public opinion or concrete proposals.</p>

<p>This choice of words generates a feeling of subtle manipulation: the reader perceives an almost predestined scenario where the only viable option is "Israel voting for Israel", as if the alternative were completely ruled out. The charged tone and the absence of critical voices create an atmosphere of inevitability that ultimately reinforces the outlet's view.</p>

<p>To spot it next time, simply look for expressions that present facts as absolutes ("revealed little appetite", "record 19 seats") and observe what information is omitted: the nuances of the campaign, the opposition's arguments, and any data that contradicts the simplified vision being sold.</p>
