<h1>Dossier of the Day: Santander pays out 1.850 billion euros in dividends this Monday amid an information blackout on China, outrage in India, and French apathy</h1>

<p><strong>Lead:</strong> While money flows with the coldness of a hydraulic current into the pockets of Santander's shareholders, the world's noise turns into static. Today we break down the mechanism: how the omission of a football match in China, the mass protests in India, and the digital vacuum in France serve as the perfect distraction for 1.850 billion euros to change hands without anyone blinking. Welcome to the circus where the show is not the performance, but the price of admission.</p>

<h2>News of the Day</h2>

<p>The hard core of our reality is financial, but the context is a mosaic of global media distortions. In Spain, Banco Santander is executing its dividend payment: 1.850 billion euros. A figure that, in traditional media, would have generated headlines of "economic success" or "shareholder reward." Here, however, we are the frame: we look at what happens when money moves while the rest of the world burns or goes cold from a lack of signal.</p>

<p>In the Asian hemisphere, Chinese state television, CCTV, has decided that Spain's LaLiga does not exist. The network has kept broadcasts suspended "following Kylian Mbappé's statements regarding a 'meme' related to Mao Zedong." As EFE reported, "none of LaLiga's matches for this Friday and Saturday were broadcast on its channels or appeared in the official schedule." Real Madrid, specifically, has disappeared from the digital map. "Nor were the matches scheduled for Sunday local night and early morning found on the network's calendar." The omission is not a technical error; it is a political framing decision disguised as sports censorship.</p>

<p>While China turns off its screens, India fills them with bodies. Thousands of demonstrators have taken to the streets of New Delhi "to demand the resignation of the chief election commissioner" over changes to voter rolls that, according to the opposition, favor the ruling party. "Hundreds of police officers, some with batons and shields, are deployed around the protest site." The tension is palpable, but international media framing tends to minimize the scale: "thousands" are mentioned, the health minister is quoted dismissing the protest as a "carefully orchestrated drama," and the depth of the institutional crisis represented by 130 million names removed from the rolls is ignored.</p>

<p>And in France, the scenario is one of digital abstinence and civic-cultural indecision. An article from Le Monde, whose full content is inaccessible to us due to technical restrictions ("JavaScript is disabled in your browser"), announces an immersion into the "undecided France" ahead of the 2027 presidential elections. The headline suggests a population that delegates: "I think I'm going to give proxy to my children and let them vote for me." It is apathy turned into narrative, the vacuum of meaning as an editorial product.</p>

<h2>The techniques in detail</h2>

<p><strong>1. Omission as a precision weapon (The Objective)</strong><br>
The cleanest and dirtiest technique at the same time. The Objective reports that "CCTV did not broadcast any LaLiga matches this Saturday." By focusing on the absence of a signal, the outlet deliberately omits the economic dimension of the exclusion. How much money is LaLiga losing? How does this affect sponsorship? It is not mentioned. The fact is presented as an exotic sports anecdote, when in reality it is a geopolitical veto. <em>How to spot it:</em> Look for what is missing. If an outlet tells you that a channel is not broadcasting something, ask: what about the money that stops flowing through that screen?</p>

<p><strong>2. Diversion through real-time noise (ABC)</strong><br>
ABC offers the Madrid-Burgos basketball game "live." The technique is distraction through immediacy. "Timothe Luwawu-Cabarrrot scores the third free throw." There is no analysis, no depth, just the uninterrupted flow of minor actions. The reader is trapped in the micro-narrative of the rebound and the basket, unable to see the forest. Madrid's "new opportunity to prolong their undefeated streak" is the hook: they make you believe that the result is what matters, when it is only the background noise upon which momentum is built. <em>How to spot it:</em> If the content is a spaghetti of data without analysis, it is designed so that you don't think, only consume time.</p><p><strong>3. The Negativity Bias as a magnifying glass (Ara)</strong><br>
Ara, from its Catalan perspective, looks at the US through a filter of structural pessimism. "Poorer and with less voice." The technique amplifies the perception of decline. "6 out of 10 Americans believe the economy has worsened under the Trump administration." The complexity of tariff policies or sectoral nuances is ignored. A picture of total decay is painted to generate a shared identity in grievance. The phrase "a real kick in the balls" from a Kansas farmer is not an accidental quote; it is the pure emotion the media outlet needs for its audience to feel the same rage. <em>How to detect it:</em> When all data, positive and negative, are colored with the same dark gray tone, you are dealing with negativity bias.</p>

<p><strong>4. The Bandwagon effect (Al Jazeera)</strong><br>
Al Jazeera frames the protest in New Delhi as a massive and urgent movement. "Thousands of protesters have taken to the streets." The technique is emotional bandwagoning: it makes you feel part of an inevitable wave. Statements by Congress leader Rahul Gandhi are cited, and the police deployment is described with "batons and shields." The goal is to make the reader feel that the resignation of the chief election commissioner is the only logical way out, ignoring the election commission's official denial of having favored any party. <em>How to detect it:</em> When the scale of the event ("thousands," "hundreds of police officers") outweighs the technical explanation of the conflict, the media outlet is asking you to jump on the bandwagon of outrage.</p>

<h2>Shared Biases</h2>

<p>What unites Chinese censorship, Spanish basketball, the US crisis, and the Indian protest? The mechanism of <strong>selective framing</strong>. All these media outlets, despite their disparate ideologies, share a pattern: the reduction of complexity to a single emotional variable.</p>

<p>In China, the variable is <em>national shame</em> (the Mbappé/Mao meme). In the US, <em>loss</em> (poorer). In India, <em>injustice</em> (electoral lists). In Spain (ABC), <em>competition</em> (unbeatability). And in France, <em>apathy</em> (procuration).</p>

<p>These biases work because they appeal to the visceral, not the rational. It is easier to sell the idea that "Mbappé insulted Mao" than to explain broadcasting restrictions due to digital rights or geopolitics. It is easier to say "the economy is doing badly" than to break down tariffs on ground meat. The mechanism does not seek to inform; it seeks to <em>position</em>. It places you in a trench: either you are with the team that doesn't broadcast, or with the one protesting, or with the one that has had enough.</p>

<p>The shared bias is <strong>financial decontextualization</strong>. While the world debates memes, protests, and basketball, Santander pays out 1.85 billion euros. Why is there no global headline about this? Because money does not generate immediate passion; it generates bureaucratic boredom. And the media lives on passion. Therefore, the dividend is the background silence, the electrical hum that keeps the operating system running while users argue about the color of the screen.</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>

<p>The mechanism, exposed. Tomorrow they will do it again.</p><p>Tomorrow, another match will be suspended, another demonstration will be an "orchestrated drama", another basketball game will be "entertainment", and another country will be "poorer". And you will keep reading, believing you are staying informed, when in reality you are being <em>framed</em>. Santander's money doesn't ask for permission; it is simply deposited. While you decide who to blame for the Mbappé meme or India's voter list, capital has already made its move. Don't try to understand the noise; look at the silence where real information should be. Right there, in the empty space between the headline and the direct quote, is where power plays its true games. Until you learn to see what is not shown, you will remain part of the scenery, not the spectator. And the scenery, my friend, does not collect dividends.</p>
