<h1>Santander drops 1.850 billion and the cover-up becomes public</h1>

<p>The bank has released a figure that sounds like a theatrical stunt, but the plot goes beyond a simple payout. What they don't want you to see is the hidden script behind the curtain.</p>
<p>This Monday, Santander announced the payment of 1.850 billion euros, a figure that, at first glance, looks like a generous gesture. However, the operation is part of a series of maneuvers aimed at diverting attention from the true financial logic. Analyzing the coverage by The Objective, ABC, Ara, Al Jazeera, and Le Monde, patterns of omission and framing emerge that paint the story to their convenience. We promise to break down the framework and show what the headlines intend to hide.</p>

<p>Behind the announcement hides a diversion strategy that turns apparent generosity into a play for influence. By emphasizing the magnitude of the payment, the media shines a spotlight on the bank's "kindness," while relegating the discussion about its true intentions.</p>

<h2>The Objective: Omission</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "Chinese state television has not broadcast any LaLiga matches since the Mbappé controversy"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://theobjective.com/deportes/futbol/laliga/2026-11-01/television-estatal-china-laliga-mbappe/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read on The Objective</a></p>

<p>The phrase that gives away the omission is "as EFE verified, none of the LaLiga matches for this Friday and Saturday were broadcast on its channels nor appeared in the official schedule." With this construction, the media outlet limits itself to pointing out the absence without explaining who made the decision or why, leaving the void to the reader's imagination.</p>

<p>That choice gives me a sense of cheap smoke: the reader perceives that something important has been hidden behind the formality of an "as verified by," as if the network were washing its hands while the truth slips away between the lines.</p>

<p>To catch it next time, it is enough to look for the absence of an active subject and a verb explaining the cause; when the text stops at "was not broadcast" and "does not appear," it is using omission as a curtain. Pointing out the gap, asking "who decided?", and comparing with international versions reveals the trick.</p>

<h2>ABC: Diversion</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "New opportunity for Madrid to extend their undefeated streak"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.abc.es/deportes/baloncesto/real-madrid-san-pablo-burgos-directo-partido-20261101123000-di.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read on ABC</a></p>

<p>The phrase that gives away the distraction technique is "Timothe Luwawu-Cabarrot sinks the third free throw." That trivial detail, repeated profusely, shifts attention away from the true focus of the headline: Madrid's supposed "new opportunity" to remain undefeated. The body of the text drowns in a chronicle of fouls, eclipsing the promise of the headline.</p><p>Reading that endless list of fouls and free throws creates a sense of saturation, as if the reader were being dragged into a sea of statistics to drown out any critical reflection on the narrative of power the newspaper wants to impose. The reader, trapped, fails to perceive the implicit agenda. The coverage becomes background noise.</p>

<p>Next time, look for the pattern: a bombardment of microscopic data that replaces analysis. If the body of the text turns into a parade of “scores the first free throw,” it is a sign of distraction. Always compare the headline and the body; if the text only lists plays, the reader must return to the main track.</p>

<h2>Ara: Negativity Bias</h2>

<p><strong>Actual headline:</strong> "Més pobres i amb menys veu: els estatunidencs després de dos anys de Trump"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.ara.cat/internacional/estats-units/mes-pobres-menys-veu-estatunidencs-despres-anys-trump_1_5895168.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read in Ara</a></p>

<p>The phrase that gives away the negativity bias is “6 out of 10 Americans consider that the economy has worsened under the Trump administration.” By highlighting the majority of negative opinions and omitting any optimistic nuance, the article feeds the sense of decline. The headline itself, with its melancholic tone, reinforces the gloomy view.</p>

<p>That choice leaves a bitter taste, as if the reader were being pushed into an abyss of hopelessness without being offered a way out. The constant reference to the “tariff squeeze” and the “kick in the balls” intensifies the anguish and reinforces the idea of a country in ruin.</p>

<p>To detect it next time, simply look for numbers exceeding 50% and expressions that underscore the worst version of events. When the text prioritizes complaint over solution, and repeats adjectives like “empitjorat” (worsened), “ofegats” (drowned), or “agreuada” (aggravated), it is applying negativity to capture attention.</p>

<h2>Al Jazeera: Bandwagon</h2>

<p><strong>Actual headline:</strong> "Thousands rally in New Delhi to demand India’s election chief resign"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/1/thousands-rally-in-delhi-demanding-indias-election-chief-resign?traffic_source=rss" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read in Al Jazeera</a></p>

<p>“Thousands rally in New Delhi to demand India’s election chief resign” is the phrase that deploys the bandwagon technique. By stating that thousands of people have joined, the media turns an isolated event into a massive phenomenon, as if popular opinion were the only one that matters.</p>

<p>This choice of words makes the reader feel trapped in an irresistible current; the idea that “thousands” are standing up suggests a collective force that cannot be ignored, provoking a sense of urgency and forced belonging.</p>

<p>To detect it next time, simply look for quantifiers that sound like “hundreds” or “thousands” and phrases that position the action as a dominant movement. If the text insists on the magnitude of participation, it is almost certain that the author is appealing to the bandwagon effect.</p>

<p>In practice, if you see phrases like “thousands of voices” or “hundreds of voices” and no contrasting data is presented, the technique is in full play, seeking to make the reader feel the pressure of not being left out.</p>

<h2>Le Monde: Framing</h2>

<p><strong>Actual headline:</strong> "N/A"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.lemonde.fr/politique/article/2026/11/01/je-crois-que-je-vais-donner-procuration-a-mes-enfants-et-les-laisser-voter-pour-moi-a-moins-de-six-mois-de-la-presidentielle-2027-plongee-dans-la-france-indecise_6803970_823448.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read in Le Monde</a></p><p>Le Monde's story is built around the phrase "<strong>JavaScript is disabled in your browser. Please enable JavaScript to proceed</strong>", which acts as an alarm signal that directs the reader toward an alleged technological fault. That small call to action frames the problem as a user failure, not the medium's, thus shifting responsibility to the public's "negligence".</p>

<p>This choice brings a bitter smile to my face: the message, so generic, is presented as a serious diagnosis, but in reality it is a smoke screen that hides any real content. The formal tone of the notice contrasts with the total absence of information, generating a feeling of subtle manipulation and "elegant censorship".</p>

<p>To detect it next time, one only needs to look for expressions that turn a technical glitch into a moral issue ("please enable...", "check your connection"). When the text becomes more of a sermon on best practices than news, it is a sign of framing that seeks to divert the reader's attention.</p>
