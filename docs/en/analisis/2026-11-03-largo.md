<h1>Feijóo and the emergency: Is Spain on the brink of the abyss?</h1>

<p>Feijóo, with the voice of a troubadour in the plenary session, declares that Spain is in a state of emergency. The public, as always, is left with the question: what on earth is hidden behind that word?</p>
<p>In the middle of the political storm, the regional leader jumps into the arena with a message that, according to him, demands immediate action. The media, as always, becomes the board where cards of fear and accusation are played. We, as an anti-media outlet, ask ourselves: is this emergency real or an image trick? Join us on this journey to untangle the truth hidden behind the smoke.</p>

<p>Beneath the cloak of alarmism, Feijóo resorts to an arsenal of rhetorical techniques: Card Stacking and Fear Appeal, which distort reality and create an echo of panic. The analyzed media outlets, from The Objective to BBC World, amplify the narrative without questioning it, while the audience becomes a spectator of the crisis itself. It is not the emergency, it is the manipulation; and we reveal it.</p>

<h2>The Objective: Card Stacking</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "López Obrador's wife received preferential treatment to obtain nationality"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://theobjective.com/espana/2026-11-03/mujer-lopez-obrador-preferente-nacionalidad/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read on The Objective</a></p>

<p>The phrase that gives away the Card Stacking technique is "an action that, according to these same sources, is not usually carried out with the rest of citizens applying for nationality." With that sentence, the media outlet chooses an isolated piece of data that suggests exclusive treatment and remains silent about any normal process that may have existed.</p>

<p>That selection produces a feeling of well-calculated cynicism in me, as if the newspaper wanted to paint power as a shadow that slips away from regulations, while the reader is left with the impression that the system is rigged in favor of a few.</p>

<p>To recognize this trick next time, one must look for the absence of counterparts: are cases where the application is resolved without hitches also cited? Is there data from other applicants that proves the normality of the procedure? If the text only shows the exception, it is building a biased stack.</p>

<h2>Libertad Digital: Fear Appeal</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "Air traffic controllers, terrified that the identity of the operator singled out by the Government will be leaked"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.libertaddigital.com/libremercado/2026-11-03/los-controladores-aterrados-de-que-se-filtre-la-identidad-del-operario-senalado-por-el-gobierno-7485565/?utm_source=rss&amp;utm_medium=feed&amp;utm_campaign=Libertad+Digital%3A+Noticias+y+opini%C3%B3n+en+la+red" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read on Libertad Digital</a></p>

<p>The phrase that gives away the fear technique is "out of respect for the operator and their family, who could suffer a public lynching." The use of the threat intimidates the reader and legitimizes the media's narrative. A strategy is perceived that turns potential whistleblowers into targets of collective retaliation, forcing the public to accept the official version without questioning it.</p><p>That choice produces a mixture of irritation and unease in me; the reader feels trapped between compassion for the worker and the terror of being singled out. The alarmist tone seeks to paralyze reflection and channel energy into defending one's own group, rather than demanding accountability.</p>

<p>To recognize it in future articles, it is enough to monitor appeals to personal fear: words like "fear," "leaking their identity," or the insinuation of a "public lynching." When the piece relies on the threat of harm to relatives or reputation, it is playing with the fear technique. Maintaining a critical gaze and asking what interests are hidden behind that pressure is the best defense.</p>

<h2>elDiario.es: Card Stacking</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "Óscar Puente faces a hostile Congress that calls for his resignation and further explanations regarding the Urda incident"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.eldiario.es/política/oscar-puente-enfrenta-congreso-hostil-pide-dimision-explicaciones-suceso-urda_1_13631514.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read on elDiario.es</a></p>

<p>The phrase that gives away the "card stacking" technique is "<em>all the parties with which he will debate in the highest parliamentary body... have already asked him to resign</em>." The text exclusively chooses voices that demand resignation, casting a shadow over any stance that defends the minister or questions the responsibility of the railway infrastructure.</p>

<p>That selection leaves me with a feeling of a pre-assembled script, as if the reader were watching a movie where the antagonist is already decided and the rest of the characters only serve to reinforce that view. The narrative becomes an echo of reproaches, without offering nuances or data that could balance the scales.</p>

<p>To recognize it next time, it is enough to look for: (1) a list of accusations without counterweights, (2) verbs that imply urgency or condemnation ("requests," "demands"), and (3) the absence of quotes presenting the version of the accused himself or of neutral experts. When the text is built as a chain of demands, it is employing "card stacking."</p>

<h2>InfoLibre: Name Calling</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "The businessman accused of scamming Madrid during the pandemic avoids the judge again, this time using Ukraine as an excuse"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.infolibre.es/política/empresario-acusado-timar-pandemia-madrid-evita-nuevo-juez-vez-ucrania-excusa_1_2239312.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read on InfoLibre</a></p>

<p>The most obvious resource of the "Name Calling" tactic is found in the expression "elusive businessman," which the media outlet itself inserts as a label loaded with contempt. That word, rather than describing a behavior, functions as a moral label that predisposes the reader to reject the subject before any nuance is offered.</p>

<p>Upon reading that phrase, the brain responds with a mixture of disdain and cynicism; the biting tone of the article becomes a sort of verbal boxing glove that strikes before the fact is even presented. The sensation is that of being guided by a narrative that has already decided the verdict, leaving little room for objective reflection.</p>

<p>To recognize this resource in future reports, it is enough to look for adjectives or nouns that turn the character into a negative stereotype without providing concrete evidence. When the text resorts to qualifiers like "elusive," "unreliable," or "cynical" instead of verifiable data, it is employing the Name Calling technique and, therefore, the information deserves a deeper critical look.</p>

<h2>BBC World: Negativity Bias</h2><p><strong>Real headline:</strong> «US strikes Iran ahead of midterm elections»</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx240mrm3g8vo?at_medium=RSS&amp;at_campaign=rss" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read on BBC World</a></p>

<p>The phrase that gives away the negativity bias technique is "US strikes Iran ahead of midterm elections". That temporal link connects a hostile action with the political calendar, creating an atmosphere of urgency and threat that captures the reader's attention. The use of the word "strikes" and the reference to Qeshm Island intensifies the image of danger, while the adverb "ahead" suggests that the event is a deliberate prelude to the political contest.</p>

<p>This choice leaves us with a sense of unease, as if the conflict were part of an electoral chess game. The reader perceives the threat as something imminent and, without realizing it, associates domestic politics with a military escalation.</p>

<p>To recognize it next time, simply look for the juxtaposition of a negative event with an election date or a political milestone. When the headline or the body of the text emphasizes chronology to magnify the threat, the negativity bias is at work.</p>
