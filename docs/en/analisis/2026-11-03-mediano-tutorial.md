<h1>Card Stacking: The art of telling the story you want the public to hear</h1>
<p>In a world saturated with data, the selection of information has become a double-edged sword. The technique of <strong>card stacking</strong> —a way of presenting only the facts that favor a specific narrative— has infiltrated political speeches, advertising campaigns, and market analyses. This tutorial breaks down what it is, how it is used, concrete examples, how to detect it, and above all, how to combat it without falling into the rhetoric that condemns it.</p>

<h2>What is Card Stacking?</h2>
<p>Card stacking is a communication strategy that consists of exclusively displaying data that supports a position, while leaving aside data that weakens it. It is a selection bias which, when combined with the visual presentation of facts, creates an illusion of consensus or solid evidence. It is not about lying, but about deliberately omitting information that might contradict the message one wishes to spread.</p>
<p>This practice relies on the psychology of confirmation: people tend to accept information that reinforces their beliefs and reject that which challenges them. By limiting the spectrum of data, card stacking takes advantage of this bias to shape public perception.</p>

<h2>How it is used in practice</h2>
<p>Users of this technique typically follow a simple pattern:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Selective collection:</strong> Only studies, surveys, or statistics that favor the desired position are gathered.</li>
<li><strong>Visual presentation:</strong> Bar charts, line graphs, or infographics that highlight strong points are used, while opposing data is hidden or presented in a less attractive way.</li>
<li><strong>Narrative framing:</strong> A story is constructed that places the selected information in a favorable context, minimizing nuances that could alter the interpretation.</li>
</ul>
<p>In the political sphere, for example, a candidate may publish an infographic showing the approval percentages of their proposals without including survey results that reveal a downward trend. In the business sector, a company may highlight revenues from the last quarters without mentioning rising costs affecting profitability.</p><h2>Real-world example: the campaign of Óscar Puente and Pedro Sánchez</h2>
<p>Óscar Puente, a candidate for the mayoralty of a major city, launched a campaign centered around the promise of "growing together." His communication team published a series of posters showing employment growth over the past five years, without mentioning the increase in the unemployment rate over the past two years. At the same time, the growth of municipal revenues was emphasized, without referring to the rise in public debt.</p>
<p>Pedro Sánchez, for his part, used a similar approach in his campaign for the provincial presidency. In a campaign video, figures showing a reduction in crime were presented, but the statistic regarding the increase in domestic violence was omitted. Furthermore, emphasis was placed on the successes of housing programs, without mentioning the delays in delivering new constructions.</p>
<p>Both examples illustrate how card stacking can give a distorted impression of reality, focusing solely on aspects that favor the candidate's narrative.</p>

<h2>How to detect it</h2>
<p>Detecting card stacking requires a critical eye and a systematic evaluation of the presented information. Below are some guidelines:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Verify the source:</strong> Does the information come from an independent source or from the promoting organization itself?</li>
<li><strong>Compare data:</strong> Look for alternative studies or reports from independent agencies that can confirm or contradict the presented data.</li>
<li><strong>Analyze the visualization:</strong> Charts that exaggerate bars or lines to give the impression of sustained growth may be indicative of card stacking.</li>
<li><strong>Review the coverage:</strong> Is negative information mentioned or is it relegated to the background? The absence of critical data is often a sign of biased selection.</li>
<li><strong>Consult experts:</strong> Market analysts and specialized journalists can offer a balanced perspective on the validity of the data.</li>
</ul>
<p>To illustrate, let's review an infographic from Óscar Puente's campaign. The graph shows a 12% employment growth over the past five years, but it fails to include the unemployment figure which, according to the local statistics office, increased by 4% over the same period. This discrepancy is a clear sign of card stacking.</p><h2>How to combat it</h2>
<p>The fight against card stacking relies on transparency, education, and regulation. Specific strategies are outlined below:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Promote media literacy:</strong> Invest in educational programs that teach citizens to critically analyze information and identify biases.</li>
<li><strong>Encourage the publication of complete data:</strong> Public institutions and companies must publish reports that include both positive and negative results.</li>
<li><strong>Establish disclosure standards:</strong> Regulatory bodies can set requirements that compel advertisers and candidates to present complete and balanced data.</li>
<li><strong>Use verification tools:</strong> Fact-checking platforms and data analysis applications can help the audience detect inconsistencies.</li>
<li><strong>Incentivize constructive criticism:</strong> The media and social networks must promote informed debate and error correction.</li>
</ul>
<p>A successful example is the transparency initiative of the Spanish Agency for Transparent Information (AEIT), which requires all electoral campaigns to publish a data report including both positive and negative indicators. This measure has significantly reduced the practice of card stacking in recent years.</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Card stacking is not an act of dishonesty per se, but rather a manipulation of perception supported by the deliberate omission of information. In an environment where information is abundant but attention is scarce, this technique can shape public opinion in a subtle yet powerful way. Detecting and combating card stacking requires a combination of critical skepticism, media literacy, and clear regulations. Only in this way can we ensure that the information reaching the masses is as complete and balanced as the reality it purports to describe.</p>
