<h1>The Framing: The president of Adif resigns from his position before Carlos Cuerpo...</h1>
<p>Today, the railway landscape is shaken: the resignation of ADIF's top official comes just as the new Minister of Transport, Carlos Cuerpo, takes the reins. In this dossier, we break down the headlines, the techniques used by the media, and the recurring patterns so that the reader can see beyond the facade.</p>

<h2>News of the Day</h2>
<ul>
<li>The president of ADIF, Luis Pedro Marco de la Peña, submitted his resignation to the First Vice President and Minister of Economy, Trade and Business, Carlos Cuerpo, one day after the resignation of former Minister of Transport, Óscar Puente (<a href="https://www.eldiario.es/economia/presidente-adif-luis-pedro-marco-pena-presenta-cuerpo-renuncia-cargo_1_13639153.html">elDiario.es</a>).</li>
<li>Carlos Cuerpo's government, on its first day, has accepted the resignation and thanked Marco de la Peña for his work, as it prepares for an initial meeting with the Secretary of State for Transport (<a href="https://www.eldiario.es/economia/presidente-adif-luis-pedro-marco-pena-presenta-cuerpo-renuncia-cargo_1_13639153.html">elDiario.es</a>).</li>
<li>El Mundo warns of a deterioration in train reliability until 2030, with forecasts of more incidents and less compliance with maintenance objectives, despite increased funding (<a href="https://www.elmundo.es/espana/2026/11/04/6aeb8622e4d4d84d628b45a2.html">El Mundo</a>).</li>
</ul>
<p>In <strong>The Objective</strong>, host Miguel Ors "ironizes" about the administration's agility and delays, while the podcast is presented as a source of certainty (<a href="https://theobjective.com/podcasts/analisis-economico-podcast/podcast-analisis-economico-espana-funciona/">The Objective</a>).</p>
<p>According to <strong>20 Minutos</strong>, PP leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo states that "people cannot be given a knot in their stomach when they get on a train," attempting to reassure the electorate in the face of railway problems (<a href="https://www.20minutos.es/nacional/alberto-nunez-feijoo-gente-no-se-le-puede-hacer-un-nudo-estomago-cuando-entra-un-tren_7057413_0.html">20 Minutos</a>).</p>

<h2>The Techniques in Detail</h2><h3>Appeal to Authority</h3>
<p>Outlet: The Objective</p>
<blockquote>“Economic Analysis Podcast | Of course Spain works. Miguel Ors ironizes in ‘Economic Analysis’ about the agility and delays of the Administration depending on the services demanded.”</blockquote>
<p>How to detect it: the use of a “podcast” format and the presence of a host with apparent credentials adds a layer of expertise that disguises opinion as fact.</p>

<h3>Alarmism</h3>
<p>Outlet: El Mundo</p>
<blockquote>“Less reliability, more unpunctuality, total non-compliance with maintenance objectives, and even a higher probability of accidents occurring.”</blockquote>
<p>How to detect it: enumeration of negative scenarios, escalating figures, and the promise of a worse future, all presented as inevitable.</p>

<h3>Negative Framing</h3>
<p>Outlet: elDiario.es</p>
<blockquote>“Luis Pedro Marco de la Peña presented his resignation from office this Wednesday to the First Vice President and Minister of Economy, Trade and Business, Carlos Cuerpo.”</blockquote>
<p>How to detect it: the news focuses on the resignation and the ex-minister’s “defense,” painting the management as the target of an attack and presenting the new official as an authority figure who “accepts” the resignation.</p>

<h3>Personalist Corruption Framing</h3>
<p>Outlet: OkDiario</p>
<blockquote>“Puente withdrew 105 million earmarked to ‘improve public transport’ to buy votes for Sánchez with his Youth Summer program.”</blockquote>
<p>How to detect it: the narrative links a budgetary decision to an alleged vote-buying scheme, creating a direct association between policy and the party's personal gain.</p>

<h3>Appeal to Fear and Reassurance</h3>
<p>Outlet: 20 Minutos</p>
<blockquote>“People shouldn’t get a knot in their stomach when they get on a train.”</blockquote>
<p>How to detect it: a metaphor is used that suggests safety while simultaneously acknowledging potential public anxiety, in order to reassure without addressing the root of the problem.</p>

<h2>Shared Biases</h2>
<p>All the outlets analyzed resort to dramatizing the railway situation, either by exaggerating risks (alarmism) or by framing the protagonists as heroes or villains (framing). The “appeal to authority” technique appears in the podcast format, while the internal “witch hunt” cited by El Mundo reinforces the idea of a hidden conspiracy. These biases work because they trigger immediate emotions – fear, indignation, or blind trust – and prevent the reader from delving into the structural data.</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>The mechanism is exposed: headlines are built with persuasion tricks that seek to shock rather than inform. Tomorrow they will do it again, but now we already know how to read between the lines.</p><hr>
