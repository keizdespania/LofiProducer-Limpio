<h1>Vox conquers Galicia: the trick behind the supposed electoral boom</h1>

<p>While headlines inflate figures, Vox celebrates two seats in Galicia as if it were the arrival of the Apocalypse. The press wallows in it, but the truth hides among the margins of manipulated charts.</p>
<p>The analyzed media outlets—from El Español to Libertad Digital—use exaggerated percentages to create a sense of inevitability that disorients the PSOE. They employ negative framing that paints the party as a shadow against the supposed strength of the PP and Vox. Furthermore, they combine self-deprecating humor and fear, hinting at a return of the independence threat. In this piece, we will unravel the play on words and data that fuels the alarm without solid proof.</p>

<p>An inflated calculation, a narrative of failure, and collective guilt become the perfect recipe to delegitimize the government. Thus, framing becomes the true weapon, and reality is relegated to the background.</p>

<h2>El Español: appeal to statistical authority (use of inflated percentages to create a sense of inevitability and urgency)</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "13 days to go: the PP has an 88% chance of being the party with the most seats, a 94% chance of being the most voted, and a 100% chance of achieving a majority with Vox"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.elespanol.com/espana/política/20261116/faltan-dias-pp-opciones-partido-escanos-votado-lograr-mayoria-vox/1003744459163_0.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read on El Español</a></p>

<p>The phrase that gives away the appeal to statistical authority is "a 100% chance of achieving a majority with Vox." That "100%" without a confidence interval is presented as a probability subject to error. In addition, the constant repetition of "chances of being the party with the most seats" reinforces the illusion of irrefutable statistics.</p>

<p>That figure hits us with a sense of inevitability that borders on the grotesque: the reader feels dragged into a current of manufactured urgency, as if the future were already written in stone and journalism were merely a campaign megaphone. Tired of propaganda, they perceive the tactic as pressure seeking to close the debate before the vote.</p>

<p>To unmask it, it is enough to look for three signs: round percentages without a margin of error, statements of "100%" or "almost certain," and the absence of independent sources to contrast the poll. If the text avoids any methodological discussion, it is most likely selling certainty instead of information. Comparing the poll with other sources, reviewing the sample, and demanding the margin of error are the antidotes.</p>

<h2>OkDiario: Negative focus and power comparison (framing), highlighting the weakness of the PSOE against the supposed strength of PP and Vox, generating an impression of the leader's failure.</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "N/A"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://okdiario.com/espana/campana-sanchez-contra-okdiario-no-da-frutos-psoe-queda-104-escanos-frente-holgada-mayoria-pp-vox-20904749" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read on OkDiario</a></p>

<p>The phrase that gives away the use of negative framing is "the PSOE stays at 104 seats compared to the comfortable majority of PP and Vox." By juxtaposing the low figure of the PSOE with the "comfortable majority" of the right-wing bloc, the text constructs an image of absolute weakness of the socialist leader.</p>

<p>To recognize this trick in future texts, it is enough to look for exaggerated figures accompanied by emotional verbs such as "regret," "fear," or "demand," and observe whether the information is presented as a moral condemnation of the reader. When data becomes an implicit sentence, the technique of fear and guilt is underway.</p>

<h2>InfoLibre: appeal to humor and fear to delegitimize regulation</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "More than 7,000 political ads bypass Meta and Google's ban in Spain"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.infolibre.es/política/7-000-anuncios-politicos-burlan-prohibicion-meta-google-espana_1_2239786.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read on InfoLibre</a></p>

<p>The phrase that gives away the tactic is "After 20 years of waiting, this great dream is starting to become a reality!". The use of a festive and almost sacred tone to announce a hospital turns the ban into a bad joke, while humor serves as a shield to hide political intent.</p>

<p>This choice of words generates a feeling of acid cynicism; the reader perceives that the message is trying to distract with laughter while selling them a hidden agenda. The contrast between the promise of hope and the harsh reality of the political campaign produces a breath-stopping mixture of irritation and skepticism.</p>

<p>To detect it next time, one must track down excessive enthusiasm in prohibited contexts. When a political ad is wrapped in overflowing praise, celebratory emojis, or phrases that sound like an anthem, it is a sign that humor is being used as a smokescreen. The golden rule: look for the incongruity between the jocular tone and the regulations being evaded.</p>

<h2>Libertad Digital: Use of negativity and sensationalism to create alarm and blame the PSOE without presenting detailed evidence.</h2>

<p><strong>Real headline:</strong> "Pedraz investigates the illegal financing of the PSOE through the SEPI Plot"</p>

<p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href="https://www.libertaddigital.com/espana/2026-11-16/pedraz-investiga-la-financiacion-ilegal-del-psoe-a-traves-de-la-trama-sepi-7491131/?utm_source=rss&amp;utm_medium=feed&amp;utm_campaign=Libertad+Digital%3A+Noticias+y+opini%C3%B3n+en+la+red" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Read on Libertad Digital</a></p>

<p>The outlet resorts to the phrase: "UCO agents and the Anti-Corruption Prosecutor's Office are investigating whether the SEPI plot could have illegally financed Pedro Sánchez's PSOE." That line, loaded with accusation without documentary backing, acts as a trigger for alarm and blame. The reader is trapped in a narrative that does not question, it only asserts, as if the truth were a mere press rumor.</p>
<p>Upon reading that sentence, adrenaline spikes and indignation takes hold of the reader. The absence of concrete evidence feels like an echo of baseless accusation, provoking a sense of injustice that spreads like a storm of unsubstantiated words.</p>
<p>To detect it next time, it is enough to look for words that drop suspicion without evidence: 'could', 'suspects', 'investigate', 'irregularity'. Also observe the absence of verifiable numerical data and the repetition of alarming terms. If the text feeds on 'hidden powers' without proof, the alarm is automatic.</p>
