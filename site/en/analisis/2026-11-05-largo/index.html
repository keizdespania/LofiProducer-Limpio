The text leaves no doubt that alarmism is lurking: the phrase "a severe deterioration in train 'reliability' up to 2030" acts as the final nail in the coffin of the reader's trust.

That turn of phrase pushes us to imagine rusted tracks and imminent derailments, provoking a mix of irritation and fear that makes the newspaper look like a prophet of doom.

To spot this trick next time, one only needs to look for inflated numbers, comparisons to catastrophic scenarios, and adjectives that sound like a legal sentence; when the headline promises catastrophe before offering a solution, the narrative is using fear as a sales weapon.

In practice, it is enough to cross-reference the data with official reports, ask what margin of error is accepted, and observe whether the text resorts to expressions like 'higher chances of accidents occurring' without backing them up with evidence; that is the sign that fear has become the main argument.

ABC: negative framing that discredits Trump and his leadership capacity

Real headline: "The Republican electoral debacle weakens Trump in appointing his successor"

Source: Read in ABC

ABC's text relies on the phrase "The magnitude of the defeat did not lead the president to acknowledge a single mistake" to paint Trump as incapable of self-criticism. That expression, loaded with judgment, turns a simple failure to acknowledge [a mistake] into a sign of absolute ineptitude.

The choice of that fragment generates a feeling of calculated disdain; the reader perceives that the newspaper is not merely reporting, but seeks to undermine the former president's authority with a tone of sarcastic superiority.

To recognize negative framing next time, one must watch out for absolute verbs, the absence of nuance, and the tendency to present a lack of self-criticism as a synonym for incompetence. When the headline and the body of the article repeat the same accusation without offering balanced data, bias is lurking. Critical reading also involves comparing with other sources and asking whether the narrative emphasizes weakness rather than concrete facts.

OkDiario: Personalistic corruption framing

Real headline: "Puente withdrew 105 million earmarked to 'improve public transport' to buy votes for Sánchez with his Youth Summer"

Source: Read in OkDiario

The phrase that unmistakably gives away the framing technique is "to buy votes for Sánchez with his Youth Summer." By placing Puente's action within an electoral exchange scheme, the media outlet transforms a simple budget reallocation into an act of political manipulation, guiding the reader to a pre-established conclusion.

This lexical choice provokes a feeling of cynicism and distrust; the reader perceives a narrative that not only informs, but judges, creating an image of the protagonists as dirty-trick strategists. The ironic tone of the text reinforces the idea that the budget is turned into campaign currency, which incites indignation without the need to delve into data.

To detect it in future articles, it is enough to look for expressions that link administrative decisions with electoral or personal intentions, especially when verbs such as "buy", "exchange" or "trade" are used alongside politicians' names. These words act as an anchor that frames the information within a narrative of self-interest.

Ara: Attribution of direct blame and emotional validation of civilian suffering due to reactive foreign policy, using the verb 'admet' to suggest that the truth was hidden and is now imposed under pressure from the ballot box.

Actual headline: "N/A"

Source: Read in Ara

The phrase that gives away Ara's tactic is "ha admès l'impacte del conflicte" (has admitted the impact of the conflict). By placing the verb "ha admès" as a late confession, the media outlet suggests that the truth was hidden and has only come to light under the pressure of the elections, fulfilling the formula of direct blame and emotional comfort for the reader.

This choice produces a feeling of subtle manipulation in me: the text seems to offer a valve of catharsis to the public by acknowledging civilian suffering as a consequence of a reactive foreign policy, while simultaneously pointing to the protagonist as the sole responsible party. The combination of admission and attribution of blame creates a narrative that feels both confessional and accusatory.

To identify this resource in future articles, one simply needs to look for verbs of belated recognition ("admet", "confessa", "reconeix") accompanied by references to the collateral damage of government decisions. When the journalist links that admission with electoral pressure, they are using the same recipe of direct guilt and emotional validation that we have dissected here.

elDiario.es: negative framing

Actual headline: "The president of Adif resigns from his position before Carlos Cuerpo"

Source: Read in elDiario.es

elDiario.es's account relies on a phrase that distills the technique of negative framing: "an insidious, fallacious, and absolutely unfair analysis".

That lexical choice plunges us into the abyss of controlled indignation, as if the reader were an accomplice to a disinformation conspiracy. The adjective "insidious" suggests deliberate manipulation, while "fallacious" and "absolutely unfair" turn any nuance into an unequivocal condemnation. The tone becomes so loaded that objectivity is diluted, leaving the public with the sensation of having been crammed into a pre-established narrative.

To recognize this trick in future readings, it is enough to track the extreme qualifiers that accompany the facts and check whether the text offers balanced data or merely reinforces a hostile view. When a description becomes excessively negative and lacks counterpoints, it is a sign of negative framing. Maintaining a critical eye and looking for sources that present the same news without emotional embellishments is the best defense.

20 Minutos: Appeal to fear and reassurance: an attempt is made to calm the public's anxiety by using a metaphor that suggests train safety prevents dangerous situations from occurring.

Real headline: "Alberto Núñez Feijóo: 'People shouldn't get a knot in their stomach when they get on a train'"

Source: Read in 20 Minutos

The text opens with the phrase that serves as the technique's banner: "People shouldn't get a knot in their stomach when they get on a train." That line, loaded with promises of safety, unfolds like an umbrella of calm that conceals the true tension of the infrastructure as a whole.

This choice of words makes me feel a mixture of disbelief and resignation. Upon reading that the president presents himself as the savior of the train cars, the irony becomes a knife that cuts through the illusion that the railway is a safe refuge. The promise turns into a mask that disguises a lack of action.

To detect it next time, it is enough to look for the promise of tranquility presented as a shield against uncertainty. If the text sounds like a siren song of security that conceals a lack of concrete data, the technique is at work. Keeping a critical eye on the metaphors that promise calm is the best defense.

InfoLibre: sensationalism and victim-blaming

Real headline: "United States punishes Trump and hands power to Democrats in the House of Representatives and the Senate"

Source: Read in InfoLibre

The phrase that gives away the sensationalist and blameworthy technique is "It was no surprise." That "it was no surprise" serves as a trick to present the result as an inevitable sentence, burdening the reader with a sense of premeditated fatalism while simultaneously implying that the "punishment" of Trump is a logical and deserved consequence, as if the electorate were paying a debt. This choice of words creates a sense of manipulation: the text does not merely inform, but rather steers the interpretation toward the idea that the Democrats "deserve" power and that the Republicans are to blame for their own defeat.

To detect it next time, one must be alert to verbs that assign value judgments ("punishes", "puts an end to") and adjectives that turn facts into dramatizations ("painted blue"). Furthermore, the presence of absolute statements ("It was no surprise") and the absence of nuance indicate that the narrative is designed to provoke an emotional reaction rather than offer a balanced exposition.

Expansión: Use of the 'authority' and 'bandwagon' technique, positioning Spanish capital as the main cause and suggesting that everyone is following this trend.

Real headline: "Hotel investment heads for a record year thanks to Spanish capital"

Source: Read in Expansión

The text becomes a carousel of names of power: “The Matutes, Calena, Blasson, and Mohari” and “RIU, the García-Baqueros,” etc. The phrase that exposes the authority and bandwagon technique is “The Matutes, Calena, Blasson, and Mohari drive hotel investment in Spain to record levels.”

That parade of titans makes me feel a mixture of curiosity and disdain. Seeing the same names repeat, the feeling that the market is an exclusive club where only the chosen ones can enter becomes almost an invitation to follow the pulse.

To spot it next time, look for the presence of names of influential figures or recognized companies, followed by verbs implying leadership or success, and the word "record" or "exceptional." If the text suggests that everyone is on board, it's a bandwagon.

This strategy doesn't sell a hotel; it sells the idea that wealth is a closed club and that success is measured by invested banknotes. When the media outlet repeats the formula, the reader ends up believing that the trend is inevitable, as if the market were a train.

Al Jazeera: manipulative irony that induces a sense of normalcy after a controversial shift

Real headline: «Mamdani: ‘it’s morning again in America’ after blue wave»

Source: Read on Al Jazeera

The phrase that gives away the manipulative irony is “it’s morning again in America.” By presenting it as a headline, the media outlet turns it into an everyday mantra that disguises the magnitude of the political turn, turning a blue wave into a simple sunrise. English here functions as a seal of global normalcy, as if the change were inevitable.

That twist of language generates a feeling of forced normalcy: the reader perceives the change as something inevitable and peaceful, as if the night had ended and everything were returning to routine, while behind it lie economic and social tensions that the text barely touches upon. Thus, the reader becomes an accomplice without perceiving the manipulation.

To detect it next time, one must look for expressions that turn controversial facts into positive clichés, observe the absence of hard data and the substitution of deep analysis for reassuring metaphors; when the headline sounds like a morning greeting, it is a sign that calm is being sold instead of truth. That pattern reveals the trap.

The Guardian: Appeal to fear when mentioning Ebola and the evacuation, generating a sense of urgency and threat.

Real headline: «British healthcare worker with Ebola evacuated from DR Congo to UK»

Source: Read on The Guardian

The text reveals the appeal to fear technique with the phrase: "Ebola is a highly contagious, severe and often fatal illness."

Upon reading it, the reader is plunged into a latent terror; the urgency feels like a breathless alarm.

Detecting it means reading the emotional charge that the words "highly contagious" carry, the combination of alarming figures, and the promise of a "high consequence" unit that sounds like a danger shield.

In practice, an attentive reader looks for phrases describing the disease with adjectives like "severe" and "fatal," figures exceeding one thousand, and mentions of high-risk units; that is the hallmark of the fear the media sells.

The effect intensifies when the media emphasizes the distance between the risk and the reader: "the risk to the UK public from Ebola remains low." This phrase creates a false security that, paradoxically, feeds the alarm.
