The President of Israel: the move that changes the board

With a firm pulse, the head of state orders the formation of a new government. This move, far from being a simple political maneuver, reveals the true architecture of power in the Middle East.

Amidst a scenario where diplomacy becomes a game of chess, the Israeli president has decided that the board will change hands. The news has shaken the international community, which closely watches every move made by the leader. In this article, we break down the process, exposing the hidden mechanisms underlying this decision, and reveal how the players are preparing for the next phase.

Behind the facade of consensus lies a network of covert negotiations, where national interests and strategic alliances intertwine like pieces of a complex puzzle. The president, with the precision of a chess master, has mobilized his allies to secure control over the political destinies that will ultimately define the course of the region.

El Español: Dehumanization and victim-blaming via the 'mafia' metaphor

Real headline: "Ayuso ahead of Nov 29: 'The union mafia is campaigning for its master Sánchez and hijacking public services'"

Source: Read on El Español

The most obvious recourse for dehumanization and victim-blaming is the mafia metaphor itself, quoted verbatim: “the union mafia is campaigning for its master Sánchez and hijacking public services.” With this imagery, the newspaper transforms the labor unions into a criminal organization at the service of a dark lord.

This lexical choice generates a sense of collective fear and artificial urgency, as if the reader were facing an internal invasion that can only be avoided by voting for the proposed figure. The reader becomes suspicious of any actor other than the designated "master."

To recognize this trick in future articles, one simply needs to look for three clues: an entity labeled as a "mafia" or "cartel," the identification of a "master" pulling the strings, and the promise that only one political option can save us. When these appear together, the discourse is constructed to dehumanize and lay blame.

Thus, the alert reader becomes skeptical, dismantles the narrative, and avoids falling into the trap of manufactured fear.

La Vanguardia: Emotional labeling and attribution of internal motivation

Real headline: "A young man fascinated by the far right: this is how the Polish Government defines the Krakow gunman"

Source: Read in La Vanguardia

The phrase that gives away the use of emotional labeling and the attribution of internal motivation is "fascinated by far-right ideologies." By using the word "fascinated," the aggressor is endowed with an almost romantic passion, while "far right" acts as a stigma that simplifies the individual's complexity.

That choice hits me like a punch of cynicism: the reader is pushed to feel repulsion and, at the same time, to accept without question that the root of the event lies in a supposed ideological obsession. The loaded tone turns the subject into a textbook monster, avoiding reflection on structural factors.

To detect it next time, it is enough to track intensifying adjectives ("very, very radical," "fascinated") and the tendency to link the action to a single internal cause. When the text replaces factual description with value judgments and motivates the reader to a predefined emotional response, it is employing the technique of emotional labeling and internal attribution.

20 Minutos: Appeal to authority and social consensus (bandwagon effect)

Real headline: "Isabel Díaz Ayuso, acclaimed in Valencia: 'Spain needs Feijóo, he is going to deliver, he is the only one who unites so many sensitivities'"

Source: Read in 20 Minutos

The technique can be seen in the phrase "We have the Pope and the punk," where the media outlet resorts to the figure of a universal authority to legitimize its message, and in "The President of the Government has called to 'vote en masse,'" which appeals to social consensus and a sense of collective urgency.

This choice makes me feel like a spectator trapped in a carousel of rhetoric, where credibility is sold at the expense of authenticity and participation is transformed into an almost dogmatic mandate, reminding us that the media has become a megaphone for opinions without the ability to question the printed narrative of the and the immediate option now.

To detect it next time, it is enough to look for quotes that cite icons of power or invoke mass mobilization without offering concrete data; those are the signs of a bandwagon and an appeal to authority disguised as analysis.

Libertad Digital: moral panic

Real headline: "Trade union flop in France: protest turnout plummets after four weeks"

Source: Read in Libertad Digital

The phrase that gives away the technique of moral panic is the one Sánchez pompously repeats: "Even Pope Leo XIV has voted." By invoking the highest religious authority as an example of civic duty, the text creates a false alarm that abstention is a social heresy, forcing the reader to fear the supposed "regression."

That choice produces a feeling of irritation and distrust in me; the speech becomes a maneuver to polarize and turn electoral apathy into a collective sin. Biting irony arises upon seeing how the popular conscience is shaken with an argument so reminiscent of a propaganda mass, rather than of serious reflection.

To detect it next time, it is enough to look for the combination of external authority (religion, science, tradition) used as a weapon to generate fear of deviation. When the text links an everyday topic to a sacred symbol and presents it as an imminent threat, moral panic is at work.

BBC World: curiosity induced through the promise of pending information

Real headline: "What we know so far after Krakow gun attack"

Source: Read on BBC World

The word that lays bare the induced-curiosity tactic is the phrase "Here is everything we know so far". By placing it at the beginning, the BBC opens the story like a half-finished notebook, leaving the reader with the feeling that the story is about to be completed.

This choice arouses a mix of tension and curiosity: the audience feels as if they were standing at the entrance of a closing door, knowing that something big is about to be revealed but unable to see it yet. The direct tone and the absence of concrete detail leave the reader in suspense.

To detect it next time, it is enough to look for phrases that hint that the information is incomplete: "until now", "so far", "stay tuned", or any construction that leaves an intentional gap. When the story is built around a promise of "more" or "coming soon", curiosity is already underway.

Bloomberg: appeal to authority and sensationalism

Real headline: "China Set to Lead World in Energy Investment in 2026, CCTV Says"

Source: Read on Bloomberg

Bloomberg's savage trick is hidden behind the phrase "CCTV Says", a label of authority that makes the statement sound as if it came from the very camera of truth. The reader, just like that, allows themselves to be swallowed by the apparent impartiality of CCTV.

That gesture makes me feel as if a police car, with its siren blaring, had crossed the line of logic. The word "Says" becomes a suit of armor for credibility that needs no backing, just a name.

To avoid falling into the same trap, next time I must look for the real source: who wrote the information? What data supports the claim? An authority without evidence is a sign of sensationalism in disguise.

Furthermore, the absence of concrete figures or independent analysis suggests that Bloomberg is more interested in impression than in the truth. A reminder that authority is not always synonymous with accuracy.

Europa Press: Appeal to authority and fear (argumentum ad verecundiam and fear of 'regression')

Real headline: "N/A"

Source: Read on Europa Press

The phrase that gives away the appeal to authority is "Even Pope Leo XIV has voted," a reference taken from a history book used as an endorsement to legitimize the urgency of voting. By invoking such a venerated religious figure, the text intends for the decision to go to the polls to feel like a moral obligation.

That choice of words generates a sense of subtle but relentless pressure: the reader perceives themselves as part of a minority that, by failing to follow the Pope's example, would be conspiring against progress. The fear of 'regression' creeps in between the irony of the quote and the promise that the far-right will crumble if everyone votes.

To detect this in future texts, it is enough to look for references to venerated figures or historical data presented as an unquestionable norm, and observe whether they are accompanied by warnings about a decadent future if one fails to act. When the narrative combines authority with an implicit threat of backward steps, the same authority-fear formula is at work.
