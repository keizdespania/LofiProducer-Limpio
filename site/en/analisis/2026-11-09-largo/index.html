Nvidia investigates its board member: the plot the media doesn't want to tell

While tech titans fight for control, an Nvidia board member disappears amid press shadows. The major newspapers howl with applause, but the real show is in the plot brewing behind the scenes.

The headlines of La Vanguardia and ABC paint Nvidia as an innocent target of a plot, while OkDiario and 20 Minutos feed the hysteria with a halo of approval. InfoLibre and Libertad Digital, with their usual confrontational bias, launch accusations that mix fear and authority. In this report, we dismantle the disinformation machinery that has turned an internal investigation into a circus of opinions, and we reveal what the media giants prefer to hide.

The trick consists of mixing the fear of losing control with the glorification of figures like Trump, to distract from the real problem: Nvidia's lack of transparency. By uncovering the framing strategy and the use of artificial urgency, we expose the facade that the media has built. The reader, armed with the truth, will no longer be an accomplice to the spectacle.

La Vanguardia: appeal to the threat of loss of control and the negative association with Trump

Real headline: "Sánchez warns that if PP and Vox govern they will bow to Trump: 'These guys swallow everything'"

Source: Read in La Vanguardia

The phrase that gives away the threat tactic is "will bow to Trump: 'These guys swallow everything'", which places the potential government as a slave to an external power and suggests the loss of Spanish sovereignty.

That lexical choice generates a mix of irritation and distrust; the reader perceives that they are being sold an apocalyptic scenario where the country becomes the backyard of a transnational far-right, provoking a visceral reaction of rejection.

To recognize it in another text, one only needs to look for two clues: first, the allusion to an "imminent danger" implying that the reader or the nation will lose control; second, the direct association with a controversial figure (Trump) to load any rival political project with negative connotations. When both are combined, the discourse becomes a game of fear and discredit.

Furthermore, the structure is usually accompanied by strong verbs like "swallow" or "bow", which intensify the feeling of submission and reinforce the narrative of "danger".

ABC: positive framing (halo effect)

Real headline: "N/A"

Source: Read in ABC

The clearest sign of positive framing is found in the phrase "barely alters the blocks for 29N," which acts as a veil of indifference against the most impactful facts. By describing the railway accident and the death of an elderly woman as mere "poll points," the text reduces them to weightless variables, creating the illusion that the electoral landscape remains unchanged and, therefore, positive for the protagonists.

This lexical choice generates a feeling of forced calm, as if the chaos were a mere mist that does not cloud the clarity of the forecast. The reader perceives a discourse that, under the guise of objectivity, softens critical events to maintain the narrative of political stability, provoking a mixture of skepticism and annoyance.

To detect this trick in future readings, it is enough to look for expressions that minimize or dismiss relevant events using adverbs like "barely," "only," or "already with." When information is presented as a static photograph that "changes nothing," it is a sign that the author is applying a halo effect to protect the image of political actors.

OkDiario: Appeal to emotion through the exaggeration of applause to generate sympathy and spectacle

Actual headline: "The Shaman and the implausible applause"

Source: Read in OkDiario

The phrase that gives away the strategy is "implausible applause," an exaggerated nod that turns any event into a spectacle of ovation. Upon reading that expression, the reader is dragged into an emotional roller coaster where reason is relegated to the background, and curiosity is fed by the promise of a show.

That resource generates a feeling of complicit camaraderie, as if you were attending a circus performance where the audience applauds without knowing why. The grandiloquent tone awakens enthusiasm, but also slight irritation, as the intention to manipulate perception through the noise of applause rather than facts is perceived.

To recognize it in the future, it is enough to watch out for the use of superlatives that describe audience reactions ("applause," "ovations," "shouts of joy") without any objective backing. When the text becomes more spectacle than information, the technique of emotional appeal through exaggeration is lurking.

20 Minutos: appealing to contrast and authority, using the contrast between Trump and trains to discredit the president and reinforce Feijóo's image as a practical, infrastructure-oriented leader

Actual headline: "Feijóo asks Sánchez to 'look less at Trump and more at the trains' and demands that Adif executives return the bonuses they have collected"

Source: Read in 20 Minutos

The phrase that gives away the strategy is "look less at Trump and more at the trains," a nod that positions the US leader as a symbol of the absurd compared to Feijóo's supposed railway common sense. By pairing the former president with an image of chaos, the media creates a contrast that discredits Sánchez and elevates the candidate as a practical authority. The article also mentions "returning the bonuses they have collected" to reinforce the image of austerity, while the contrast with Trump serves as a moral anchor.

This choice brings a sardonic smile to my face; the reader perceives a veiled mockery intended to convince them that the solution lies in the rails and not in international politics. The pompous tone of the text, laden with references to "30 years" of service, seeks to present Feijóo as the sole savior of everyday chaos.

To recognize it in future texts, one must look for exaggerated comparisons, names of international figures used as scapegoats, and the insertion of verbs such as "review... inch by inch" that confer unsubstantiated expert authority. Detecting these formulas makes it possible to dismantle the illusion of objectivity that the media outlet attempts to project.

InfoLibre: Contrast effect and sensationalism: weekly variability is highlighted to generate doubt and alarm.

Actual headline: "N/A"

Source: Read on InfoLibre

The phrase that gives away the technique is "the campaign that changes its script every week." That fragment, with its rhythm of "changes... every week," is designed to create the sensation of an unstable roller coaster that never settles, feeding the reader's doubt and shock.

This choice makes me feel as if I were watching a political soap opera, where each new episode surpasses the previous one in drama; the reader is trapped in the expectation of the unexpected, with a constant alarm that prevents them from resting.

To detect it next time, it is enough to look for words that imply constant variability ("every week," "script twists," "they've never had time"), and observe how the text relies on that instability to generate impact, rather than presenting consolidated facts.

Libertad Digital: Existential urgency and collective victimization through the dehumanization of elapsed time.

Actual headline: "N/A"

Source: Read on Libertad Digital

The phrase that gives away the technique is "Morocco invaded Spain through Ceuta, three months ago, with Sánchez's complacency." The use of the verb "invaded" along with the temporal reference "three months ago" creates an urgency that forces the reader to feel that the danger is latent and that the government has failed.

This choice produces a mixture of irritation and skepticism in me: the exaggeration of the threat and collective blame turn the text into a distorted mirror of reality, where the reader is dragged into an atmosphere of constant alarm that fuels polarization.

To recognize it in the future, it is enough to look for three clues: a pressing chronology ("X time ago"), inflated numbers meant to frighten, and the assignment of blame to a collective "other." When the narrative relies on these resources to dramatize facts, it is employing existential urgency and collective victimization as a rhetorical weapon.

Le Monde: appeal to authority

Actual headline: "Mobilisation sociale: pour Sébastien Lecornu, la semaine de toutes les négociations"

Source: Read on Le Monde

In the excerpt left to us by Le Monde —«JavaScript is disabled in your browser»— not a single phrase can be glimpsed that serves as a pin for the appeal to authority, that tactic of wrapping information in the mantle of a supposed expert or a venerable institution. The absence of a quote such as “according to the minister” or “claims the committee chair” leaves the reader with the sensation of having stumbled upon a blank page, a technical excuse that, in a sarcastic tone, seems to tell us that true informative power is hidden behind a wall of code.

To avoid falling into the trap of searching for authority where none exists, it is enough to scrutinize the text for words that legitimize the voice of the article: verbs like “claims,” “declares,” or the names of high-ranking positions. When those clues disappear, as happens here, the narrative lacks the tool of authority and, therefore, becomes an empty echo. Next time, simply ask: who is speaking and under what title? If the answer does not appear, the appeal to authority is absent.

Al Jazeera: Negativity Bias

Real headline: «New Delhi blanketed in toxic smog after Diwali fireworks»

Source: Read on Al Jazeera

By describing the city as “blanketed in toxic smog,” the media outlet already triggers negativity bias, as the word “blanketed” suggests an inescapable layer that envelops the inhabitants in an invisible threat.

That choice leaves me with a sensation of suffocation and urgency, as if the air itself were an enemy attacking without mercy; the reader is dragged into a scenario of constant alarm, with no room for positive nuances.

To recognize the tactic in future texts, it is enough to look for adjectives that intensify the danger (“toxic,” “severe,” “blanketed”) and turn objective data into sensory images of horror; when the headline turns a measurement into an apocalyptic scene, the bias is underway.

The reader, by absorbing that atmosphere of alarm, tends to overestimate the real severity of the problem and underestimate any mitigation initiative; the key is to contrast the AQI figure with official ranges and ask whether the coverage is comprehensive or isolated.

The Guardian: Fear (Alarmism)

Real headline: «Collision of two trains in Egypt kills at least seven»

Source: Read on The Guardian

The Guardian wields the scalpel with surgical precision to embed the final peg: «catastrophic mismanagement and poor maintenance». This is not a simple human error, but a systemic pathology. By labeling the infrastructure as «common», they normalize chronic negligence. That phrase does not inform; it passes sentence. It makes you feel like a helpless spectator before the collapse of an old building that everyone knew was going to fall, yet no one did anything. The fear here is not of the crash, but of the idea that collapse is the natural state of things. It is lukewarm alarmism, wrapped in the asepsis of British English, that leaves you with a knot in your stomach knowing that the disaster was not an accident, but an appointment to which the system arrived on time.

To detect it in the future, don't just look at the headline. Look for the adjectives that qualify the root cause. If the text doesn't just say "accident," but adds "history of disorder" or "lack of maintenance," they are constructing a narrative of inescapable fatality. The technique is not to shout, but to whisper incompetence as if it were a geological fact. When you see them turn a one-off failure into a permanent condition of the environment, you will know that they are not looking to inform, but rather to make you assume that chaos is the price of admission. That is the real alarm: that we are no longer surprised when things fail.

Europa Press: appeal to collective action and a sense of urgency

Real headline: "Sánchez insists on shielding pensions and raising the minimum wage, and calls to vote 'en masse' to 'go much further'"

Source: Read in Europa Press

The technique is blatantly revealed in the quoted phrase: "vote en masse". That call to collective action is not a simple slogan, but a mobilization maneuver that turns the electorate into an army of volunteers, as if every vote were a shot in the battle against the "right" and the "far-right."

Upon hearing that phrase, the immediate response is a mixture of enthusiasm and a certain unease: the message sounds like a battle cry, where the fear of losing becomes the driving force behind participation. You feel the pressure not to be left out of the cause, the urgency of being part of something grandiose, and the promise that, if you don't act now, the future will be ruined.

To detect this tactic in the future, look for expressions that turn individual action into a collective imperative ("en masse," "all together," "the people" in the plural). Also observe the presence of a clearly defined enemy and the implicit urgency ("in the next four years," "to go much further"). If the piece plays with these elements, it is likely appealing to mobilization and collective urgency.

The mechanism, exposed

Tomorrow they will do it again. And you will already know where to look. That is The Framing.
