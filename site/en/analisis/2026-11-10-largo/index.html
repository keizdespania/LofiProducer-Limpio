The Supreme Court reveals that 180,000 children of alleged 'ni' are at risk

The Supreme Court has dropped a confession that shakes the family landscape. These are 180,000 children of alleged 'ni' who, according to the judiciary, could be in a precarious situation.

This announcement comes after years of debate on the State's responsibility in protecting minors. Official data, revealed in a press conference, paints an alarming picture that demands an urgent response. In this issue, we break down the figure, analyze the implications, and expose the network that has allowed this number to remain hidden. Get ready to discover how this shadow of invisibility was built and what consequences it will have for society.

The truth is hidden behind a tangle of bureaucracy and silence. What seemed like an isolated number turns out to be the tip of an iceberg that threatens social cohesion. Only transparency and immediate action can reverse the course.

elDiario.es: negative framing and negativity bias

Real headline: "Sánchez asks the Judiciary to investigate payments to Koldo García to make him lie against the Government"

Source: Read on elDiario.es

The headline already carries the weight of negative framing: "Sánchez asks the Judiciary to investigate payments to Koldo García to make him lie against the Government." The phrase "to make him lie against the Government" places the reader in a conspiracy scenario, insinuating a malevolent intent before presenting any evidence. That lexical twist is the unmistakable sign of the framing technique, as it transforms a simple complaint into a latent threat, forcing the audience to react with alarm.

That choice leaves me with a feeling of raw mistrust, as if the media outlet were trying to load the emotional scales before the reader can weigh the facts. Verbs like "lie" and absolute adjectives intensify the perception of danger, creating an atmosphere of urgency that clouds objectivity. Next time, it is enough to detect the presence of implicit judgments in the first sentence, the repetition of loaded terms, and the absence of verifiable data; those are the indicators of a negative framing ready to trigger the public's automatic reaction.

InfoLibre: Identity association and victimization

Real headline: "Sánchez asks to investigate payments to Koldo García: 'Inda financed his defense to defame us and bring us down'"

Source: Read on InfoLibre

The resort to identity association and victimization is revealed in the phrase "Inda financed his defense to smear us and make us fall." By mentioning Inda as an external aggressor, the media outlet positions the PSOE as the innocent victim of aggression, creating a community of readers who feel part of that struggle.

This lexical choice generates a sense of indignation mixed with pride of belonging; the reader perceives a threat that justifies defending their own group. The tone, laden with drama, incites an emotional reaction and reinforces the idea that the government is the target of an unfair conspiracy. The reader, without realizing it, becomes an accomplice to the dramatization.

To recognize this trick in future texts, it is enough to look for the formula "X acts against us" followed by a call to unity. When the narrative replaces objective facts with a tale of personal attack, it is employing the same strategy of identification and victimization.

El Independiente: normalization of protest through denial (minimization)

Real headline: "This is not a strike"

Source: Read in El Independiente

The most obvious clue of normalization-minimization is in the phrase "this is not a strike." By explicitly denying the category of the event, the media outlet places the protest outside the legitimate framework, turning it into a trivial spectacle. That denial is the hallmark of the strategy: stripping the conflict of its weight so that the reader perceives it as a parlor game.

This lexical choice hits me like a slap of cynicism: the text becomes a parade of sarcasm that dilutes the urgency of the strike and transforms it into a mere parlor curiosity. The reader, instead of feeling empathy, gets trapped in irony, as if the struggle were a piece of cheap theater.

To spot it next time, simply look for any denial that re-frames the protest as "a gathering," "a masquerade," or "a minuet." If the text replaces the word strike with leisure metaphors, it is normalizing the protest and diminishing its seriousness. Keep your guard up and always ask: what is being undermined?

Al Jazeera: appeal to anger (emotional)

Real headline: "Anger as ICE agents carry out arrest in New Jersey supermarket"

Source: Read in Al Jazeera

The phrase that gives away the appeal to anger is “Anger as ICE agents make arrest in New Jersey grocery store”. By placing the word “Anger” at the beginning, the outlet already directs the reader toward a visceral reaction before offering facts. The adjective is not descriptive; it is an implicit command to feel rage.

That choice pushes us into a defensive posture, to imagine a scene of confrontation and to fuel resentment against authority. Next time, it is enough to look for words that precede the main noun and function as emotional qualifiers: “anger”, “outrage”, “fury”. If the headline starts with one of those triggers, the text is probably built to ride an emotional rollercoaster before objective information has a chance to settle.

The subheadlines repeat “Anger” and use verbs like “storming” or “confronted”, amplifying the chaos. To counteract this, read the body of the article and look for data without emotional qualifiers.

BBC World: sensationalism

Actual headline: "N/A"

Source: Read on BBC World

The phrase that distills sensationalism is "alleged Chinese spy". By using the word “alleged” alongside “spy”, the BBC packages a rumor with the weight of an accusation that, without conclusive evidence, sounds like a spy thriller rather than balanced reporting.

This trick leaves me with a sense of unease: the news feels like a chess move where every piece is moved with the goal of provoking, not informing. The reader is dragged into a narrative of suspicion that, although plausible, lacks substance and feeds on a morbid curiosity for scandal.

To detect it next time, I look for words that act as tension "catalysts"—“alleged”, “spy”, “targeted” or “groundless”—and observe whether the text surrounds them with concrete data or simply uses them as a garnish for drama. If the piece of information is presented without context or verification, it is a sign that the outlet is playing with emotion rather than facts.

The Guardian: alarmism

Actual headline: "Deadly floods hit Iraq as thunderstorms sweep across region"

Source: Read on The Guardian

The passage that gives away the alarmist resource is the phrase “torrential rain fell over a short period”, which condenses the catastrophe into an image of uncontrollable and sudden rain. In addition, the text insists that “severe thunderstorms swept across the region”, reinforcing the idea of an unstoppable wave.

That choice generates a feeling of overwhelming immediacy, as if the reader were on the verge of an imminent catastrophe; the fast pace and the numbers exceeding expectations (“80mm … versus 30mm”) fuel an anxiety that goes beyond mere information. The reader is dragged along by a narrative that seems to prescribe chaos, without offering alternatives or calm contexts.

To recognize this trick, it is enough to look for three indicators: intensifying vocabulary (torrential, severe, sweeping), comparisons that highlight the deviation from the expected, and the juxtaposition of distant data (storms in Iran, storms in Europe), which broadens the global threat and raises emotional pressure.

Le Monde: Appeal to fear

Real headline: "Midterms 2026: 'We, the Americans, can no longer continue to engage in the world with our military power alone'"

Source: Read in Le Monde

When trying to read the Le Monde article about the 2026 Midterms, the browser displays a disabled JavaScript message. The actual text from the outlet is not available, so we cannot point to a specific word or phrase that evidences the appeal-to-fear technique. However, the headline itself—"We, the Americans, can no longer continue to engage in the world with our military power alone"—suggests a concern about military dependency, a typical clue of the fear strategy: insinuating that the absence of another source of power will lead to undesirable consequences.

This choice generates a sense of urgency and latent threat, even without using explicit words of violence. To detect this technique in the future, look for expressions that promote the idea that a decision or policy could trigger an impending crisis, and observe whether the author uses contrasts between the proposed action and a negative scenario, without directly mentioning violence. The key lies in the insinuation of risk and implicit urgency.
