Dossier of the day: SpaceX manages to return Starship to the platform after almost a day

While humanity looks up at the sky with mouths open, hoping a cathedral-sized rocket won't turn to ash, the media are busy with a different kind of explosion: verbal ones. Today, the return of Elon Musk's Starship is not just an engineering feat; it is the mirror where editors project their traumas, fears, and agendas. They are not reporting physics; they are reporting politics. And in this circus, the only thing flying high is the algorithm. Here is the dissection of the mechanism.

News of the day

The cold, precise technical news is that SpaceX has managed to have its Starship spacecraft return to the launch pad after a flight of nearly a day. It is a milestone in orbital reusability. But if you read the mainstream press, that rocket does not appear. Instead, you find Pedro Sánchez comparing himself to the Gestapo, Feijóo promising a million homes, and Zelensky negotiating territories with the Americans. Physical reality has been crushed by political reality. The hard data of aerospace engineering has been relegated to the background by the deafening noise of the Spanish electoral campaign and the geopolitics of Ukraine.

Because the public doesn't want to know how a Raptor engine works; they want to know who is going to steal their vote. As OkDiario points out in an exercise of historical hyperbole: "The entire Sánchez Gestapo against me." And while that explodes, 20 Minutos reminds us of the urgency of bricks and mortar: "It is Spain's biggest problem." The cohesion between the sky and the asphalt is nil, but the emotional connection between fear and the click is perfect.

The media and their techniques

The media are not mirrors; they are distorted prisms. Each filters reality through its own ideological and commercial lenses. They are not looking for the truth; they are looking for a reaction. Here is the game board, exposed without mercy:

The techniques in detail

Let's break down the tools of the trade. They are not mistakes; they are strategies.

1. Victimization and demonization through historical analogy
Medium: OkDiario
Quote: "The whole Sanchista Gestapo against me" and "The whole Sanchista Gestapo against me Adolf Hitler created the Gestapo in April 1933" Note: The actual quote in the body of the text is "The whole Sanchista Gestapo against me".
How to spot it: Look for comparisons between current political figures and historical totalitarian regimes. If a journalist compares a minister to extremist secret police, they are not analyzing; they are insulting. This technique aims to dehumanize the opponent, making rational debate impossible. It is the "you are not a politician, you are a monster" approach.

2. Appeal to fear (exaggeration of the problem)
Outlet: 20 Minutos
Direct quote: "It is Spain's biggest problem"
How to spot it: Identify the extreme superlative applied to a systemic problem. By labeling housing as "the biggest problem," artificial urgency is generated. Citizens stop thinking about solutions and begin to feel panic. Fear paralyzes and, at the same time, looks for a savior. In this case, the savior is the party that promises to "build one million homes" without explaining how.

3. Determinism and self-fulfilling prophecy
Outlet: Ara
Direct quote: "The majority of the right would climb today to 192 seats" and "Vox reaching 20% of the vote, a historic result"
How to spot it: Observe how polls are presented as accomplished facts. The use of verbs in the near future or present tense for electoral results that are still far off creates a narrative of inevitability. If the media says "the right is winning," left-wing voters, terrified, mobilize out of fear, or right-wing voters, confident, abstain. The prediction becomes the driver of the outcome.

4. Disqualification through negative labeling
Outlet: Libertad Digital
Direct quote: "Feijóo champions management in the face of Sánchez's 'squandering'"
How to spot it: Look for pejorative adjectives that summarize administrative complexity. "Squandering" is not a technical term; it is a moral label. By labeling government action as "squandering," any debate on efficiency, cuts, or priorities is invalidated. Politics is reduced to a struggle between "those who manage" and "those who throw money away."

5. Fear (alarmism)
Outlet: The Guardian
Direct quote: "After the world’s hottest summer since records began in 1850, the death toll from extreme weather in 2026 is already in the high tens of thousands"
How to spot it: Pay attention to the accumulation of catastrophic data without nuance regarding the capacity for adaptation or mitigation. The list of disasters (fires, floods, heat) without context for solutions generates a sense of impending apocalypse. The reader is not left informed, but terrified. Terror is an excellent seller of subscriptions and existential angst.

6. Delegitimization through moral labeling
Outlet: InfoLibre
Direct quote: "The time of the 'ladies of' has come to an end"
How to spot it: Look for expressions that reduce a social group to an obsolete stereotype. "Ladies of" implies passivity, dependence, and a lack of independent agency. By saying that this time "has come to an end," a portion of the female electorate that might still vote differently is invalidated. It is a subtle way of saying: "If you don't vote like me, you are a relic of the past."

Shared biases

What do OkDiario, The Guardian, and Libertad Digital have in common? Nothing in their content, everything in their form. They all operate under the same shared bias: emotional polarization. No serious media outlet can afford nuance, because nuance doesn't generate clicks. Anger, fear, and outrage are high-octane fuel for the attention economy.

The pattern is clear: a real problem is taken (housing, climate, the conflict in Ukraine, public management) and a distorted magnifying lens is applied to it. Context is stripped away, extremes are amplified, and reality is presented as a binary battlefield. You are either with the "managers" or the "wasters." You are either among those who save the planet or those who set it on fire. There is no middle ground. This mechanism works because it exploits human heuristic cognition: we prefer simple, Manichaean narratives to the gray, boring complexity of reality. They sell us drama because the truth is too slow.

Conclusion

Starship has landed. It is a significant technical achievement that demonstrates the viability of orbital rocket reuse. But in the press, that rocket is just a backdrop for political theater. While the engineers celebrate the engine's success, the writers celebrate the success of their manipulation. They have turned a scientific fact into a mirror for their own hatreds and fears. They haven't informed you; they have conditioned you. The mechanism has been exposed. Tomorrow, they will do it again.
