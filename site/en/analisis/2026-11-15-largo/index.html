SpaceX gets Starship to return to the platform: what does it imply?

The American giant has succeeded in bringing its most ambitious spacecraft back to Earth. The world watches, with amazement and suspicion, this episode that promises to rewrite the limits of space travel.

After a test flight that ended in a dramatic fall, SpaceX has demonstrated its ability to correct errors and move forward. This return to the platform not only validates the company's engineering, but also opens the door to more ambitious interplanetary missions. In this article, we break down the technical process and examine the implications for the aerospace industry and the future of human exploration.

The mechanism that allows Starship to land without firing the descent engines is a combination of exhaust propulsion and attitude control via high-precision gyroscopes. This approach, which has been the subject of speculation, demonstrates the maturity of next-generation rocket propulsion technology. With each attempt, SpaceX not only refines its product, but redefines the narrative of space conquest.

OkDiario: victimization and demonization through historical analogy

Actual headline: "The entire Sanchista Gestapo against me"

Source: Read on OkDiario

The headline "The entire Sanchista Gestapo against me" and the phrase “Pedro Sánchez also has his own Gestapo” are the warning sign that betrays the strategy of victimization and demonization through historical analogy.

That device gives me a mixture of irritation and distrust, as if the author were trying to turn a political dispute into a shadow conflict, equating rivals with the executioners of the last century.

To recognize it in future texts, one only needs to watch out for three clues: exaggerated comparisons with totalitarian regimes, the use of loaded vocabulary that places the adversary at the extreme of evil, and the presentation of the narrator as the sole innocent under siege.

The reader, upon encountering that mixture of history and politics, is pushed to accept a Manichean view where the other is a monster and the author is the sole survivor. That simplification, in addition to fueling polarization, prevents any serious analysis and leaves the public trapped in a spiral of disinformation.

20 Minutos: appeal to fear (exaggeration of the housing problem to generate urgency)

Actual headline: "Feijóo says that if he wins, housing management will be handled from a vice presidency: 'It is Spain's biggest problem'"

Source: Read on 20 Minutos

The appeal to fear is revealed in the phrase "it is Spain's biggest problem," a hyperbole that transforms a complex issue into an imminent threat and forces the reader to accept the urgency of the discourse.

This lexical choice generates a feeling of pressure, as if the country were on the verge of collapse due to the lack of housing; the reader is dragged into a state of anxiety that makes them seek the solution promised by the candidate, even if it is a vague promise.

To spot this trick next time, it is enough to watch out for superlatives and absolute qualifiers intended to dramatize reality ("the biggest," "the worst," "catastrophic"). When a news story resorts to these extremes without providing comparative data, it is employing the appeal to fear to create a narrative of artificial urgency.

Ara: Determinism and self-fulfilling prophecy

Real headline: "N/A"

Source: Read in Ara

The phrase that gives the technique away is "the most likely outcome is that it will be left without parliamentary representation." This statement turns a statistical projection into an inevitable certainty, as if the future were already written and the reader only had to accept it. By presenting the numbers as an inescapable destiny, the media outlet reinforces the idea that the right will win.

This choice leaves me with the feeling of a self-fulfilling prediction: the text suggests that Sumar's downfall is sealed, which incites the reader to view the future as immutable and to justify the right's narrative as inevitable. By citing exact figures, the article creates a false objectivity that masks its ideological charge.

To detect this next time, one must look for words that turn probabilities into absolute truths, pay attention to superlatives ("historic," "most likely"), and note the absence of nuances indicating uncertainty. Also, look out for the lack of contrasting sources and the reliance on a single study. When discourse becomes a prophecy, the reader must ask: what actual data supports this certainty?

Libertad Digital: disqualification through negative labeling and polarizing contrast

Real headline: "Feijóo claims good management against Sánchez's 'wastefulness' and kicks off his path toward Nov 29 in Galicia"

Source: Read in Libertad Digital

Libertad Digital's text betrays its disqualification strategy in the phrase "incompetence, neglect, and having deteriorated public services." By placing three negative adjectives in a row, the media outlet turns Sánchez's government into a monster of inefficiency before the reader has even read the rest.

This accumulation of epithets generates a feeling of weariness and cynicism: the reader perceives that the story does not seek to inform, but to hurl verbal darts that polarize reality. The tone becomes aggressive, almost theatrical, and the public is trapped between provoked anger and the automatic defense of their own side.

To detect this in future articles, it is enough to watch out for lists of negative qualifiers and the absence of nuance. When a piece groups together several derogatory adjectives without offering contrasting data, it is using the technique of polarizing labeling. The key is to ask: what is said about the opponent and what is omitted about one's own side?

The Guardian: Fear (alarmism)

Real headline: "Choking fires, deadly heat and devastating floods: 2026 in extreme weather"

Source: Read on The Guardian

The Guardian resorts to alarmism with the phrase "the death toll from extreme weather in 2026 is already in the high tens of thousands," which suggests an imminent catastrophe without offering comparative data.

This choice produces an overwhelming sense of urgency, as if the planet were on the verge of collapse and only we, the readers, could stop the avalanche.

To recognize the trick next time, it is enough to look for quantitative superlatives ("high tens of thousands"), figures without verifiable sources, and the repetition of apocalyptic adjectives that turn information into sensationalist entertainment.

The average reader becomes a spectator of a climate horror movie where every month carries a "fourth-hottest" label and every figure becomes an omen. The tactic consists of mixing real data with adjectives that exaggerate the threat, forcing the brain to accept chaos as the norm. If you spot the formula – raw data + superlative + call to immediate action – you will have disarmed the trap.

Le Monde: Appeal to emotional cohesion through the description of "cortèges hétéroclites mais déterminés" (heterogeneous yet determined processions), which generates a feeling of unity and determination in the face of perceived chaos.

Real headline: "Manifestations du 14 novembre : à Paris comme à Lyon, des cortèges hétéroclites mais déterminés"

Source: Read on Le Monde

The height of journalistic irony is revealed in the phrase "cortèges hétéroclites mais déterminés," where Le Monde turns the protest into a parade of colors and wills. With this choice, the reader perceives a collective force, a pulse that cuts through urban confusion and transforms into a chant of determination. Language, far from dramatizing the chaos, turns it into a backdrop that highlights the unity of the protesters. This technique, the appeal to emotional cohesion, relies on the juxtaposition of "hétéroclites" and "déterminés," evoking diversity and firmness at the same time. When the next headline mentions "various" and "determined" or "disorderly yet firm," you will know the media outlet is trying to forge a sense of community in the face of disorder. Thus, the word "déterminés" becomes the anchor of a narrative that, with a touch of sarcasm, promises that the crowd will not dissipate but rather become a single voice.

InfoLibre: Delegitimization through moral labeling and appeal to obsolescence

Real headline: "El tiempo de las ‘señoras de’ se ha acabado: el PSOE vuelve a disputar el voto de las mujeres el 29N"

Source: Read on InfoLibre

The phrase that gives away the delegitimization technique is "El tiempo de las ‘señoras de’ se ha acabado" ("The time of the 'ladies of' is over"). With this moral label, the media outlet boxes an entire segment of the citizenry into a framework of dismissive condescension, as if their only function were to serve as a scapegoat for progressive discourse.

That lexical choice gives me a feeling of calculated cynicism: the reader is pushed to feel that the "other" is obsolete, that their ideas are archaic, and that, therefore, the only logical way out is to align with the official narrative. The tone leaves no room for ambiguity; the moral label acts like a hammer blow that silences any nuance.

To spot this strategy next time, it is enough to look for two clues: a derogatory label that groups together a collective ("ladies of", "far-right") and an assertion of authority "it's not an opinion, polling says so." When both elements appear, the piece is constructed to delegitimize rather than inform.

BBC World: Framing of Betrayal

Real headline: "US has floated Ukraine ceding Donbas for Nato membership, Zelensky says"

Source: Read on BBC World

The phrase that reveals the technique of framing betrayal is “The Americans raised a question: 'What if Ukraine joins Nato? Would Ukraine withdraw from Donbas?'”. By placing the doubt within the narrative itself, the media turns the idea into an insinuation of betrayal, suggesting that the future ally would be sacrificed for military power.

This choice leaves me with a sense of unease, as if a conspiracy were being hatched at a marble table, with the promise of security in exchange for the homeland. The tone of the question suggests a double standard that makes confidence in Western diplomacy and global international loyalty tremble.

To detect it immediately, look for phrases that present a hypothetical condition followed by a territorial or value loss. If the text turns the proposal into a "test" of loyalty, the framing of betrayal is likely being applied.

Europa Press: Polarizing comparison (creation of a moral contrast between "good politics" and "bad manners", "involution" and "chainsaw" to generate identification with the subject and rejection of the adversary)

Real headline: "Sánchez claims 'good politics' against the 'bad manners', 'involution' and 'chainsaw' of PP and Vox"

Source: Read on Europa Press

The most obvious resource of the polarizing technique appears in the phrase "good politics" versus "bad manners", "involution" and "chainsaw", which the text itself repeats as if it were a mantra.

That choice of vocabulary cuts the discourse into two antagonistic worlds: one heroic, clean, and reasonable, and another savage, clumsy, and destructive. The reader is dragged into a position of moral superiority without the need for arguments, as if the PSOE were the only beacon of sanity in the middle of a jungle of clumsiness. Furthermore, the chainsaw metaphor suggests indiscriminate violence that fuels collective hysteria.

To recognize it in future articles, it is enough to watch for three signs: the juxtaposition of loaded adjectives, the repetition of opposing pairs, and the absence of intermediate nuances. When the text turns rivals into synonyms of chaos and its own block into a synonym of progress, the polarizing comparison is working at full throttle.
