The CNI reveals: The government prepares for an overflow of power

The CNI has warned the Government about a threat that endangers the very fabric of power. Experts claim the situation is critical and requires an immediate response.

While the global press focuses on international crises, the shadow of internal overflow looms over Spanish politics. Confidential documents reveal that the intelligence agency perceives a risk of overflow that could destabilize control mechanisms. The news sparks a debate on the legitimacy of state intervention and the protection of sovereignty.

The overflow process is based on the mobilization of state resources and coordination with European entities. Specialists warn that the speed of action will be decisive in containing the threat. The reality is that power faces an invisible crisis that only the government can halt.

El Mundo: appeal to fear (alarmism)

Real headline: "The CNI informed the Government of the risk of overflow in the Canary Islands following the crisis in Ceuta"

Source: Read in El Mundo

In the body of the article, the phrase that gives away the resort to alarmism is "the highest record of arrivals in the three decades of the history of the Canary Islands route." That expression, accompanied by the CNI's warning of a "risk of overflow," turns data into an imminent threat.

This choice generates a sense of pressing urgency, as if the archipelago were on the verge of a human avalanche. The reader, bombarded by record figures and the idea of an overflow, moves from informational curiosity to visceral unease, and the text becomes a trigger for collective fear.

To recognize this trick in future pieces, three signs must be monitored: the use of superlatives ("the highest record"), the insertion of words that evoke catastrophe ("risk of overflow"), and the juxtaposition of numerical data with an emergency narrative. When they appear together, the message does not seek to explain, but to provoke fear.

Ara: appeal to authority and the normalization of action. There is an appeal to the perception that the EU, as a regulatory and responsible entity, is taking a necessary and legitimate measure, reinforcing the idea that deportation is an acceptable solution.

Real headline: "Five EU countries will create a migrant deportation center in Rwanda"

Source: Read in Ara

The phrase that gives away the appeal to authority technique is “We welcome this initiative and have closely followed the preparatory work of the five member states,” as it positions the EU as the legitimate arbiter endorsing the measure, creating the illusion of institutional consensus.

That choice produces a feeling of bureaucratic déjà vu, as if the reader were being dragged into accepting without question a policy that has already been “approved” and “valued” by the higher powers, reducing the controversy to a mere administrative procedure.

To spot it next time, one simply needs to look for expressions that delegate responsibility to organizations or leaders (“the EU approves,” “the chancellor says,” “we welcome”), and observe how the text replaces argumentation with reference to authority, normalizing the action as an inevitable and reasonable step.

The Objective: Reframing defeat as the continuation of the threat

Real headline: "N/A"

Source: Read on The Objective

The phrase that gives away the framing is “bolsonarism is still alive.” By placing the electoral defeat right next to the supposed immortality of the threat, the text turns a clear loss into an ominous continuity, maintaining narrative tension.

That lexical choice generates a feeling of unease, as if Lula's victory were merely a blink in a night that remains dark. The reader perceives that, despite the triumph, the shadow of the past does not dissipate, which fuels an atmosphere of constant alert and “change without change.”

To detect this in future reports, one simply needs to look for two patterns: first, the mention of a positive event (victory, progress) immediately followed by a reference to a persistent force (“is still alive,” “does not surrender,” “remains latent”). Second, observe how the author uses that force as an axis of continuity, transforming the opposition's defeat into a threat that never dies.

elDiario.es: Appeal to guilt and fear, using negative association with Israel to delegitimize both candidates.

Real headline: "Gabriel Rufián: 'Israel pays Abascal and Israel pays Silvia Orriols'"

Source: Read on elDiario.es

The fragment that gives away the tactic of guilt and fear is the phrase “Israel pays,” quoted verbatim in both the headline and the body of the article. That expression, taken out of context, serves as an axis to paint the candidates as instruments of a supposed foreign agenda.

That choice of words generates a feeling of indignation mixed with a subtle paranoia, as if the reader were being dragged into a signaling game where guilt is assigned without evidence and fear is fueled by the suspicion of hidden influence. The tone provokes a visceral reaction that eclipses substantive debate.

To detect this resource in other pieces, one simply needs to watch out for three indicators: first, the direct association of a rival with a foreign power without citing verifiable sources; second, the absence of concrete data to support the accusation; and third, the clear intention to generate alarm instead of offering reasoned arguments. Maintaining a critical eye makes it possible to cut off the discourse before it turns into disguised propaganda.

La Vanguardia: sensationalism and fear of immigration

Real headline: "Five countries create in Rwanda the first center to deport migrants outside the EU"

Source: Read in La Vanguardia

The most revealing feature of sensationalism lies in the phrase "first center to deport migrants outside the EU", which turns a complex policy into a sci-fi scene. By placing the word "first" at the beginning, the text creates the illusion of spectacular novelty, while the verb "deport" sounds like a definitive punishment, reinforcing the fear of the "other".

This choice gives me a sense of unease and of being dragged into a narrative that seeks to provoke fear rather than inform. The grandiloquent tone transforms migrants into pawns in an experiment, which generates an immediate emotional reaction and a perception of imminent threat, without offering concrete data to support the announced urgency.

To recognize this trick in future articles, it is enough to look for words that promise "first", "innovative" or "laboratory", and verbs that suggest drastic action ("deport", "expel"). When the text resorts to these formulas to dramatize a measure, it is a sign that it is playing with sensationalism and fear instead of presenting a balanced explanation.

InfoLibre: Appeal to guilt and authority

Real headline: "Abascal's partner collects the social heating bonus for vulnerable families"

Source: Read in InfoLibre

In the body of the article, the phrase that gives away the technique is "joins, through his wife, the long list of politicians who collect this subsidy." By placing Abascal's partner alongside well-known names from the PP and Vox, the text invokes the authority of those positions and loads the text with guilt by implying that they are part of a privileged club.

This lexical choice provokes a feeling of calculated indignation: the reader is dragged into a scene where the political elite repeats the same move, while the most disadvantaged remain in the shadow. The ironic tone, which underlines the "aid designed for vulnerable families", reinforces the idea that injustice is presented as an accepted norm.

To recognize this resource in future readings, it is enough to look for two clues: the mention of high-ranking figures acting as endorsement and the insertion of verbs that assign moral responsibility to the protagonist. When the text points out "the long list of politicians" or "the aid designed for...", it is combining authority with guilt to provoke an automatic reaction.

20 Minutos: Induction of administrative panic

Real headline: "N/A"

Source: Read in 20 Minutos

The text resorts to the technique of administrative panic induction by using the phrase "look for alternative transport." This call to action, accompanied by the image of a paralyzed train, creates a scenario of urgency and makes the reader feel that chaos is just around the corner, with no real alternative.

The immediate effect is an anxiety that seeps through every line: "paralyzes the train" and "is expected to have a large turnout." The reader is left with the feeling that the system is about to collapse and that the only way out is to follow the operators' advice. The urgency becomes a constant noise that leaves no room to breathe.

To spot it next time, look for phrases that combine authority with a call to action that implies a threat. Observe the patterns: "based on observed facts," "recommend," "paralyzes," "expects." When the narrative relies on official data but uses it to feed fear, you are already dealing with administrative panic induction. Stay critical and verify whether the threat is real or just an excuse.

BBC World: appeal to fear

Real headline: "Five EU countries announce migrant deportation centre in Rwanda"

Source: Read on BBC World

The text opens with the phrase: "subject to deportation," which is not a simple fact, but a reminder of a terrifying destination, of the constant threat hanging over those who are left undocumented. Upon reading it, the reader feels like a spectator in a bureaucratic horror movie.

The use of that phrase creates an atmosphere of claustrophobia, of urgency, of fear of the loss of identity, of the threat of an uncertain destiny. The phrase makes the audience perceive politics as a mercilessly devouring machine.

When you see terms that describe inevitable consequences with no room for choice, such as "subject to deportation," "no return," or "boost removals," the appeal to fear technique is already at work. Look for words that sound like orders or implicit threats, and that provoke a visceral reaction. And above all, observe how the tone shifts from informative to alarmist when the word "removals" is mentioned, as it sounds like a mandate.

Libertad Digital: sensationalism and alarmism through the exaggeration of the figure and the severity of the irregularity

Real headline: "Anticorrupción investiga "graves irregularidades" en la adjudicación de contratos de Defensa por más de 6.000 millones"

Source: Read on Libertad Digital

The phrase that gives away the sensationalist technique is "more than 6 billion," as the number is presented as a colossal figure without specifying its context or its actual weight within the total Defense budget. The adjective "serious" before "irregularities" intensifies the perception of catastrophe, even though the investigation itself is still ongoing and without definitive conclusions.

That choice leaves me with a feeling of exaggerated urgency, as if the State were on the verge of financial collapse. The untrained reader is dragged into a rollercoaster of alarmism that prevents them from calmly analyzing the facts. The style seeks to capture attention before reason has a chance to intervene.

To recognize this trick in the future, just ask: what percentage does that figure represent of the total spending? Are the numbers accompanied by verifiable sources or are they hidden behind phrases like "according to confidential documents"? In addition, watch out for the presence of alarmist adjectives and the absence of concrete data; they are the signature of media exaggeration.

The Guardian: Fear (Alarmism)

Actual headline: "Five EU states announce plans for immigration 'return hubs' in Rwanda"

Source: Read in The Guardian

The Guardian employs alarmism by citing the phrase "a grave moral failing" and by promising that the initiative "will help curb illegal migration and dismantle the traffickers' business model." The use of such loaded terms elevates the sense of immediate crisis, as if Europe's stability were at stake and the only solution were a return center in distant Rwanda.

This choice stirs a mix of indignation and alarm in the reader. Upon hearing "grave moral failing," the public feels as though they are facing an ethical catastrophe, which intensifies the urgency and the fear of losing fundamental values.

To detect this in the future, simply look for words that evoke extreme urgency and collective guilt: "grave," "moral failing," "cure illegal migration," "dismantle the traffickers' business model." When the text resorts to these qualifiers without offering concrete data, it is a clear sign that the author is playing with fear to shape public opinion.

Al Jazeera: Fear and Dramatization

Actual headline: "Gunfire erupts in occupied West Bank settler attack"

Source: Read in Al Jazeera

Al Jazeera fires up its narrative with the phrase "Gunfire erupts in occupied West Bank settler attack," which acts as a linguistic puff of gunpowder. The verb "erupts" suggests an unexpected explosion, while "occupied" loads the area with a political charge that instantly places the reader in an atmosphere of tension and vulnerability.

That choice produces a feeling of latent alarm, as if danger were just around the corner, forcing the public to imagine an imminent escalation. The tone becomes almost cinematic, and the reader is dragged into an atmosphere where uncertainty becomes the protagonist.

To recognize this trick in future reports, simply track explosion verbs ("erupts," "blasts," "explodes") and qualifiers that load the territory ("occupied," "under siege"). When the headline becomes a shot of adrenaline, the piece is usually designed to ignite fear before offering balanced data.
